<!-- prompt: Why are there two high tides a day when the Moon only passes overhead once? -->
Because tides come from the *difference* in the Moon's gravitational pull across the Earth, not from the pull itself.

**The key idea: tidal force is differential**

The Moon's gravity is stronger on the side of Earth facing it and weaker on the far side, since gravity falls off with distance. Compare each part of Earth to the Earth's center:

- **Near side:** pulled more strongly than the center, so the ocean bulges toward the Moon.
- **Center:** the reference point.
- **Far side:** pulled less strongly than the center, so the center is effectively pulled *away* from the far-side water, leaving it behind as a second bulge.

The result is two bulges on opposite sides of the Earth, roughly aligned with the Earth-Moon line.

**Why this gives two high tides a day**

Earth rotates beneath these two bulges. In one rotation, a given coastline passes through both, so you get two highs and two lows.

**Why it's about 24h50m, not 24h**

The Moon orbits Earth in the same direction Earth spins, so Earth has to rotate a bit extra (about 50 minutes) to catch up to the Moon's new position. That's why high tides arrive about 50 minutes later each day, and the interval between successive highs is roughly 12h25m.

**A common misconception**

The far-side bulge is often explained as "centrifugal force from the Earth-Moon system rotating around its common center of mass." That's a convenient shorthand, but the differential-gravity explanation is cleaner and accounts for both bulges without needing it.

**Real-world caveats**

- The Sun also raises tides, about 46% as strong as the Moon's. At new and full moon they line up (spring tides, larger); at quarter moons they partly cancel (neap tides, smaller).
- Coastline shape, ocean basin size, and latitude can make some places see only one high tide a day (e.g., parts of the Gulf of Mexico) or have very unequal highs.
