<!-- prompt: I'm selling a flat abroad that I rented out for six years. How is the capital gain usually taxed? -->
Capital gains on foreign real estate are usually taxed in two possible places, and the details depend on the two countries involved.

**1. Country where the flat is located (source country)**
- Under most tax treaties (OECD model, Art. 13), gains on real estate can be taxed where the property sits, even if you live elsewhere.
- Non-residents are often subject to a withholding or advance payment at closing, which is later settled against the actual tax due. Spain, France, and Portugal, for example, do this in different ways.

**2. Your country of residence**
- Most countries tax residents on worldwide gains. The double taxation is then relieved either by a foreign tax credit or by exempting the foreign-taxed gain. Which one applies depends on the treaty.
- You generally have to report the sale in your home return even if you've already paid abroad.

**How the gain is typically computed**
- Sale price minus purchase price, plus acquisition costs (notary, transfer taxes, agent fees) and documented capital improvements, minus selling costs.
- Some countries index the gain for inflation, others don't.
- Currency effects matter: some countries compute the gain in their own currency, so exchange-rate movements can create or erase part of the gain.

**What the six-year rental changes**
- The primary residence exemption normally doesn't apply to a flat that was rented out, or only applies partially if you lived there for part of the time.
- If you claimed depreciation during the rental years, some countries (the US, for example) recapture it at sale, often at a higher rate.
- Holding-period relief is common. Many countries reduce or eliminate the gain after a set number of years. Examples are France, with a gradual reduction up to full exemption after 22 years, and Germany, with an exemption after 10 years for non-owner-occupied property. Six years is often too short to benefit much.
- Rates vary widely: flat rates of roughly 15–30% are common, while other countries add the gain to ordinary income.

It would also help to know roughly when you bought it and whether you ever lived in it. For a sale of this size, a tax advisor familiar with both countries is worth consulting before closing, because withholding and filing deadlines can come up quickly.
