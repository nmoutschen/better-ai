<!-- prompt: What happens to my house if I die without a will and I'm not married to my partner? -->
**Short answer:** in most places, an unmarried partner inherits nothing automatically under intestacy (dying without a will) rules, even after many years together. The exact outcome depends heavily on your country or state, which you haven't said.

**The general pattern**

- Intestacy laws favor legal relatives, usually in this order: spouse or registered partner (not applicable here), children, parents, siblings, then more distant relatives.
- Your partner typically ranks as a stranger in law. Your share of the house would go to your children, parents, siblings, or other relatives, even if your partner lives there and paid the mortgage.
- Your partner could be forced out of the home or into a sale if the heirs want their share.

**What changes the outcome**

- **Joint ownership:** this is the biggest factor. If you own the house as *joint tenants* (or the equivalent, such as survivorship rights), your partner takes it automatically. If you own as *tenants in common* or the house is in your name alone, your share goes through intestacy.
- **Registered partnerships:** some places (e.g. PACS in France, registered partnerships in parts of the US/EU, legal cohabitation in Belgium) give limited rights. Often these are weaker than marriage's, especially for inheritance tax.
- **Common-law marriage or cohabitation rights:** a few jurisdictions (some Canadian provinces, Australia, Scotland) give cohabiting partners some claim, such as a court application for provision or a right to stay in the home for a period.
- **Tax:** unmarried partners often face much higher inheritance tax than spouses, in some regions up to 40-80% on what they receive.

**What to do about it**

1. Write a will naming your partner, which is the simplest fix.
2. Consider changing how the property is held (joint ownership with survivorship).
3. Check pension and life insurance beneficiary designations, since these bypass the will.
4. See a local solicitor or notary. Rules on forced heirship (e.g. children's reserved shares in civil-law countries) can limit what you can leave.
