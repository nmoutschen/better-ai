<!-- prompt: Why did the Western Roman Empire fall? Historians seem to disagree. -->
Historians disagree mostly because the question bundles several different ones: what counts as "fall," how much was internal decay versus external pressure, and whether it was inevitable. Here are the main camps.

**1. Military and external pressure (the "murder" thesis)**
Barbarian migrations and invasions, especially the Huns pushing Goths and others across the frontiers, overwhelmed the western army. The defeat at Adrianople (378), the crossing of the Rhine (406), the sack of Rome (410), and the Vandal conquest of North Africa (429–439) cost the West its richest tax base. Peter Heather and Bryan Ward-Perkins are the best-known modern proponents. Ward-Perkins points to the sharp archaeological collapse in pottery, building, and coinage as evidence that this was a real catastrophe, not a smooth transition.

**2. Internal political and fiscal weakness**
Civil wars drained armies and money. Usurpers, weak emperors, and powerful generals (Stilicho, Aetius, Ricimer) turned the state into a contest among factions. The loss of tax revenue meant fewer troops, which meant more territorial loss, a downward spiral. The Eastern empire survived partly because it was richer, more urban, more defensible (Constantinople), and less exposed.

**3. Economic and social decline**
The older view (Gibbon, and later Marxist and Rostovtzeff-style readings) emphasizes heavy taxation, depopulation, reliance on slave or tied labor, and a top-heavy bureaucracy. Many historians now see this as overstated, since the 4th century was fairly prosperous in many regions.

**4. Environmental and disease factors**
Kyle Harper (*The Fate of Rome*) argues that climate shifts and pandemics (the Antonine and Cyprianic plagues, later Justinian's plague in the East) weakened the empire's demographic and fiscal base. This is influential but debated, as the evidence is hard to tie directly to western political collapse.

**5. Transformation, not fall**
Peter Brown, Walter Goffart, and the "Late Antiquity" school argue that Roman institutions, law, and culture were gradually adapted by the successor kingdoms. On this view "fall" is a misleading frame, and 476 (when Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus) was a minor event. Goffart's "accommodation" thesis suggests barbarians were settled within Roman systems rather than conquering them.

**6. Cultural and religious explanations**
Gibbon blamed Christianity for sapping civic virtue. Few serious historians accept this now; more see the Church as a stabilizing institution that outlasted the state.

**Where things stand**
Most scholars today accept a blend: the West faced unusually severe external pressure *and* was politically brittle enough that it couldn't absorb it. The real argument is about weighting. Heather and Ward-Perkins stress the violence and rupture. Brown and Goffart stress continuity. Harper adds an environmental layer.

If you want a starting reading list, I'd suggest Heather's *The Fall of the Roman Empire*, Ward-Perkins's *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization*, and Brown's *The World of Late Antiquity* for the contrasting views.
