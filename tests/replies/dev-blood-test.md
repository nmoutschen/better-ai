<!-- prompt: My blood test shows high ferritin but normal iron. What could that mean? -->
High ferritin with normal serum iron is common, and it usually does **not** mean you have too much iron. Ferritin is both an iron-storage protein and an "acute-phase reactant," meaning it rises with inflammation even when iron stores are normal.

**Common causes**
- **Inflammation or infection**, including recent illness, and chronic conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or lupus
- **Fatty liver (MASLD/NAFLD) and metabolic syndrome**, which is one of the most frequent causes of mildly to moderately raised ferritin
- **Alcohol use**, which raises ferritin and can injure the liver
- **Other liver disease**, such as hepatitis or liver injury
- **Obesity, insulin resistance, or type 2 diabetes**
- **Kidney disease**
- **Hyperthyroidism**
- **Certain cancers or blood disorders**, which are less common
- **Recent intense exercise** or repeated blood transfusions

**Less common but important**
- **Hereditary hemochromatosis or other iron overload.** This is usually suggested by high **transferrin saturation** (often above 45%), not just serum iron. Serum iron alone fluctuates a lot and can look normal, so transferrin saturation is the more useful number.

**Questions a doctor will likely ask or test**
- What's the transferrin saturation (iron ÷ TIBC)? If it's normal, iron overload is much less likely.
- Are CRP/ESR (inflammation) elevated?
- Are liver enzymes (ALT, AST, GGT) normal?
- What about fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids, and thyroid function?
- How much alcohol do you drink, and have you been sick recently?
- Is there a family history of hemochromatosis or liver disease?

**Possible next steps**
- Repeat the test in a few weeks, fasting and when you're well
- Get transferrin saturation, CRP, liver panel, glucose, and a full blood count
- If transferrin saturation is high, ask about HFE genetic testing
- If the cause stays unclear, imaging or specialist referral may be considered
