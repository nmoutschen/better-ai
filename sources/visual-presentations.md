# Visual presentations: How an AI agent should turn text or data into a short sequence of visuals

Research findings for the show-better rewrite  
Prepared for Nicolas · Search date: 8 November 2026

The evidence supports a mini-presentation built from **separate panels the reader steps through, each with a sentence headline that states its point, and one visual that makes the same point prominent**. Polish raises trust and memory more than understanding, and readers' preferences are a poor guide to what they learn. So the agent should judge its output by what the reader can answer afterwards, not by how it looks. The strongest support is for segmenting content (a review of 10 experiments) and for titles steering what readers recall (several chart studies). Most other findings rest on one study each, and no study tests a presentation generated by an AI agent for a reader with no presenter.

**Scope.** This report extends `sources/visual-representation-of-information.md` and does not repeat it. That report covers when to use prose, a table, a chart or a diagram, the encoding ranking, placing labels next to what they describe, decoration, axis truncation, arrow meaning, colour and contrast, text alternatives, and checking generated chart code. This report covers five further questions:

1. How to split content into a sequence of panels.
2. How to make each panel's main point clear.
3. Whether visual design quality affects comprehension, recall or trust.
4. How to turn prose into a chart or diagram.
5. What is known about automatically generated slides and visual summaries.

**Status.** The research stopped at the `medium` level: no high- or medium-priority question is still open. Four questions are contested (section 8). One low-priority question, on hard-to-read fonts, was left open. I checked the quotes of 45 of the board's 85 claims against their pages, including every claim behind the rules in section 1. Two claims could not be checked (section 9). The research board is in `.research/visual-presentations/`.

**Reading level of the evidence.** Most sources were read as abstracts or through a fetch tool's extraction, not as full papers. Exceptions read in full or in long verbatim parts: Blokzijl and Andeweg (2006), Mahar et al. (n.d.), SlidesGen-Bench (Yang et al., 2026) and PPTAgent (Zheng et al., 2025).

## 1. Rules the evidence supports

| Candidate rule | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Split the content into separate panels the reader moves through at their own pace** | People learn more deeply from learner-paced segments than from one continuous unit: 10 of 10 tests, median effect size 0.79 (Mayer & Pilegard, 2014). In a crowdsourced study (N = 180), a slideshow layout of text and charts gave better comprehension than a vertical scrolling layout (Zhi et al., 2019). | Strong for segmenting; moderate for slideshow over scroll (one study) |
| **Give each panel a headline that states its point as a full sentence** | Students taught with sentence-headline slides averaged 79% correct against 69% for phrase-headline slides. They scored significantly higher on 7 of 15 questions and significantly lower on 2 (Alley et al., 2006). In a 110-student study, assertion-evidence slides (sentence headline plus visual evidence) gave better comprehension, fewer misconceptions and lower perceived cognitive load than default PowerPoint slides (Garner & Alley, 2013). | Moderate: one research group, slides with a speaker, several changes bundled |
| **Make the headline match what the visual shows** | Readers' recall of a chart's message aligned more often with a slanted title than with the chart itself (Kong et al., 2019). Titles and supporting text should convey the message, and redundancy between them helps (Borkin et al., 2015). | Moderate: consistent across chart studies |
| **Make the main point the most prominent feature of the visual, then emphasise it in text** | When a caption described a low-prominence feature, readers reported the chart's high-prominence feature as the takeaway instead (Kim et al., 2021). Captions help readers remember the features they describe (Zhu et al., 2022). | Moderate: mostly line charts |
| **Annotate the visual where the point is; annotation is not clutter** | Heavily annotated charts were not penalised, and some kinds of information are best placed in the title, others closer to the data (Stokes et al., 2022). Cues that link text and picture gave a small-to-medium gain (r = 0.17), mainly for learners with low prior knowledge (Richter et al., 2016). | Moderate: one study plus a meta-analysis of 27 studies |
| **Repeat the same structure across panels** | Sequences that use parallelism, the same structure from one visual to the next, gave memory and rating benefits (Hullman et al., 2013). | Weak: one study, abstract only |
| **Show all of a panel at once; do not reveal its parts by animation** | Students who saw static slides scored higher than those who saw parts revealed by animation: 81.11% against 65.81% in the paper's table; the body text reports 81.98% against 71.43% (Mahar et al., n.d., N = 93). | Weak, and contested: see section 8 |
| **Use visual polish for trust and memory, not as evidence of understanding** | Beauty, but not actual misleadingness, raised trust in graphs (Lin & Thornton, 2021, preprint). Embellished charts were described as accurately as plain ones and recalled better after two to three weeks (Bateman et al., 2010). Colour and image-style bars raised credibility; hand-drawn fonts and cartoon-style bars lowered it (Song et al., 2025). | Moderate for trust and memory; no study on slide design quality |
| **Do not judge the output by how much the reader likes it** | Visual slides were rated better, but text slides scored higher on the immediate test, 64.1% against 59.5%, and the gap was gone after a week (Blokzijl & Andeweg, 2006, N = 237). Verbatim on-screen text got the highest ratings for understanding, while slides with less text gave better applied comprehension (Fenesi & Kim, 2014). | Moderate: several small single studies, same direction |
| **Extract the structure first, then draw it, then check it against the text** | Generating systems first infer a table from data-involved text before drawing a chart (Zhang et al., 2024), or extract a process graph and then check its logic against the wording of the text (Ying et al., 2026). The number of example texts, the specificity of definitions and the rigour of format instructions were key to accurate extraction (Neuberger et al., 2024). | Moderate: system papers, not reader studies |
| **Check generated panels for missing content and wrong values first** | In SlidesGen-Bench, 19.1% of quiz answers drawn from generated slides were wrong. Missing content caused 66.3% of those errors and wrong values 31.7% (Yang et al., 2026). PPTAgent's authors report overlapping elements and say it does not use visual information to refine slides (Zheng et al., 2025). | Moderate: author-run benchmarks |

## 2. Splitting content into panels

Segmenting has the strongest support of any finding in this report. People learn more deeply when a message comes in learner-paced segments rather than as one continuous unit: the rule held in 10 of 10 tests, with a median effect size of 0.79 (Mayer & Pilegard, 2014). The cause given is essential overload from lessons that are fast and complicated.

For screen layouts, one experiment compared a slideshow with a vertical scrolling page. Participants did significantly better on comprehension with the slideshow. Recall was better with the slideshow only when the text and the chart were linked, meaning that selecting a sentence highlighted the matching chart element and the reverse (Zhi et al., 2019).

**No source tests how much one panel should hold.** The common limit of about four items per panel comes from Cowan's (2001) estimate that working memory holds about four chunks. That estimate is about memory, not slides, so a four-item limit is an inference. A rule on panel size would be a stated design inference, not a finding.

The terms in sources differ. A systematic review describes a slideshow as a way to explain data to an audience, not to analyse it. It defines an infographic as graphics, images and text combined in one visualization, scrollytelling as storytelling combined with scrolling, and data comics as an emerging genre (Schröder et al., 2023). Segel and Heer (2010) identified genres of narrative visualization. A short HTML mini-presentation is closest to a slideshow.

## 3. Making each panel's main point clear

### 3.1 Headlines

A headline that states the slide's main assertion as a sentence increased recall in one geoscience course: 79% correct against 69% (Alley et al., 2006). **The gain was uneven**: significant on 7 of 15 questions, and significantly worse on 2. A later study with 110 engineering students found better comprehension, fewer misconceptions and lower perceived cognitive load for assertion-evidence slides than for PowerPoint defaults (Garner & Alley, 2013). That comparison changed several features at once, and both studies come from one research group, with a speaker present.

A critique argues that the control slides were poorly designed and that a speaker can state the main idea aloud instead of the headline (Farkas, 2010). **I could not verify this critique's quotes** (section 9). Its second argument does not apply to a page read without a speaker, where only the headline can state the point.

### 3.2 Titles steer what readers take away

Readers spend the most time on a visualization's text, above all its title (Conner-Simons, 2015, reporting Borkin et al., 2015). When a title was slanted against the chart, readers' recall of the message aligned more often with the title than with the chart, and most still saw the chart as impartial (Kong et al., 2019). A headline therefore controls what the reader remembers, so a headline that does not match the visual misleads.

### 3.3 Emphasis and annotation

When both the chart and its caption emphasised a high-prominence feature, readers took that feature as the takeaway. When the caption described a low-prominence feature, readers relied on the chart and reported the prominent feature instead (Kim et al., 2021). Captions helped readers remember the features they described (Zhu et al., 2022), but in that paper's results a caption did not significantly increase takeaways about the primary feature of single-line charts.

Heavily annotated line charts were not penalised, and readers preferred them. Some kinds of information worked best in the title, others closer to the data (Stokes et al., 2022). A meta-analysis of 27 studies found that cues linking text to the matching part of a picture gave a small-to-medium gain (r = 0.17), mainly for learners with low prior knowledge (Richter et al., 2016).

### 3.4 Text on a panel read without a speaker

**No controlled study tests how much text a slide should carry when it is read without a speaker.** Practitioners distinguish a document of slides that is handed out and read from a deck that is projected and watched. They pair words and images on the first but give no tested limit (Duarte, n.d.). Word counts per slide, such as an average of 55 words for social scientists, describe practice, not effect (Wageningen University and Research Centre, 2015). Fenesi and Kim (2014) found that narration with images and little text beat narration with verbatim text on applied comprehension, but that design has narration, which a page does not.

## 4. Design quality, trust and memory

Visual appeal changes trust and memory more than measured comprehension.

- **Trust.** Beauty, but not actual misleadingness, causally raised trust in graphs (Lin & Thornton, 2021, preprint). Ratings of credibility, understanding and reliability depended on both a visualization's design and its source (Pandey et al., 2023). Colour and image-style bars raised credibility, while hand-drawn fonts and cartoon-style bars lowered it (Song et al., 2025). Posts with high aesthetic appeal were rated more credible, while production quality alone did not change credibility (Computational Multi-Modal Communication Lab, n.d.).
- **Memory.** Embellished charts were described as accurately as plain ones and recalled significantly better after two to three weeks (Bateman et al., 2010). Colour and recognisable human-related objects increased memorability (Borkin et al., 2013).
- **Comprehension.** No study found tests the design quality of slides or multi-panel pages. The only typography result is one poster: font disfluency did not change paragraph comprehension (Tahiyah et al., 2024).

**Polish therefore cuts both ways for an agent.** A polished page makes its content more trusted whether or not the content is correct. The agent's checks on content (section 6) matter more as the page looks better.

## 5. Visual summaries, ordering and reveal

### 5.1 Do visual summaries beat reading the text?

The results depend on what is measured.

- **Speed.** LLM-generated tables and mind maps cut comprehension time by 42.9% and 31.9% compared with text, with no loss of accuracy (Jain et al., 2024). The same paper reports that current models struggle to generate structured output.
- **Retention.** Infographic summaries of medical articles were preferred and needed less mental effort than text abstracts, but delayed retention did not differ (Martin et al., 2019).
- **Learning.** Students who read a science comic had the highest learning gains (Science Societies, 2024, reporting a study I did not read).
- **Placement.** A graphical overview read before a hypertext improved comprehension of difficult texts. One read at the end lowered comprehension of easier texts (Salmerón et al., 2009).

### 5.2 Ordering panels

The one direct study of sequence found memory and rating benefits for sequences that use parallelism, the same structure from one visual to the next. It also proposes ordering visuals by the cost of each transition for the audience (Hullman et al., 2013). Its ranking of transition types could not be read. **No source tests whether an overview panel should come first or last** in a short sequence; Salmerón et al. (2009) is the closest evidence, and it concerns hypertext.

### 5.3 Revealing content step by step

The results split by context (section 8). Revealing visuals in step with a voiceover raised test scores (Ito & Ichikawa, 2026, read through a press release). Revealing slide content by animation lowered scores against static slides (Mahar et al., n.d.). Scrollytelling matched static and text formats on comprehension while raising engagement in one preprint (Méndez & Such, 2026), and showed no comprehension difference in another (Chen et al., 2026). A meta-analysis of animation against static graphics found a small advantage, g = 0.226, larger when the system set the pace (Berney & Betrancourt, 2016), but animation of a process is not the same as revealing parts of a slide.

## 6. Turning prose into a chart or diagram

### 6.1 Extract, draw, check

Generating systems split the work into steps. ChartifyText first prompts an LLM to infer a table from data-involved text, then draws a chart with added encodings and short text (Zhang et al., 2024). A process-graph system extracts a graph, checks its structure, then checks that its flow logic matches the wording of the text, repeating the checks as a loop (Ying et al., 2026). For extracting process information, the number of example texts, the specificity of definitions and the rigour of format instructions were key to accuracy (Neuberger et al., 2024).

### 6.2 Which content suits which form

Few (2004) classifies quantitative messages into seven types (nominal comparison, time series, ranking, part-to-whole, deviation, frequency distribution and correlation) and recommends a graph form for each. The guide concerns numbers, not prose, and reports no testing. When participants sketched graphs for verbal descriptions of data, they used bars for discrete comparisons and lines for trends. The strength of this convention depended on the communicative situation (Zacks & Tversky, 1997). Doc2Chart selects a chart type with a heuristic-guided module before generating code, and was scored against automated metrics, not readers (Jain et al., 2025).

**No source classifies relations in prose, such as cause, sequence or hierarchy, and tests a diagram form for each with readers.**

## 7. Automatically generated slides

Evaluation has moved from reference-based scores (Fu et al., 2022) to LLM judges, quiz tests and rubrics. The most documented failure is content, not appearance. In SlidesGen-Bench, 2,499 of 13,023 quiz answers drawn from generated slides were wrong (19.1%): missing content caused 66.3% of the errors and wrong values 31.7% (Yang et al., 2026). PPTAgent's authors report occasional overlapping elements and say the system does not use visual information to refine slide design (Zheng et al., 2025). PresentBench argues that holistic judgments are too coarse (Chen et al., 2026b).

Automatic judges agree with humans less than humans agree with each other. On aesthetics, SlidesGen-Bench's metrics reached a Spearman correlation of 0.71 with human ratings, against 0.57 for an LLM-as-judge and 0.85 between human annotators (Yang et al., 2026).

**No study tests whether readers learn more from generated slides on a knowledge test.** Students rated AI-generated slides as similar in quality to instructor slides and could not reliably tell them apart (Leinonen et al., 2026). Auto-Slides participants gave self-report ratings of understanding, not test scores (Yang et al., 2025).

## 8. Where sources disagree

- **Design quality.** Embellishment raised credibility and memory in some studies and lowered credibility in others, depending on its kind. Production quality alone had no effect. All of these studies use single charts, not sequences.
- **Visual summaries.** Structured summaries made reading faster without loss of accuracy, but infographic summaries did not improve retention, and an overview helped or hurt depending on its position and the text's difficulty.
- **Preference and learning.** Every study read found that preference did not track learning (Blokzijl & Andeweg, 2006; Fenesi & Kim, 2014; Martin et al., 2019; Clegg et al., 2024). The question stays contested only because a 2024 report of better recall with infographics was seen in a search result and not read.
- **Step-by-step reveal.** Reveal in step with narration helped; reveal by animation on lecture slides hurt; scrollytelling made no difference to comprehension. No study tests self-paced reveal without narration.

## 9. What the research could not find

| Gap | Effect on the skill |
|---|---|
| No study of how many items or words one panel should hold | A panel-size limit is a design inference from working-memory research |
| No controlled study of slide text read without a speaker | Text rules for a self-read page extend findings from slides with a speaker |
| No study of design quality for slides or multi-panel pages | Design rules rest on single-chart studies |
| No test of overview first versus last in a short sequence | Ordering rules rest on one hypertext study |
| No tested mapping from relations in prose to diagram forms | The skill must state which form it picks for which relation as a design choice |
| No study of learning from AI-generated slides | Checks must test content against the source; looks and ratings are not evidence |
| Farkas (2010) quotes not verified: the PDF text was garbled and no local PDF tool was available | The critique of the sentence-headline studies is reported, not relied on |
| Blocked or unreadable: Rey et al. (2019) segmenting meta-analysis, Mayer (2008), Deslauriers et al. (2019), the full texts of Hullman et al. (2013), Segel & Heer (2010) and PresentBench | Reading them could settle panel size, ordering and the preference question |

## 10. Method

Six worker agents researched 13 questions on a shared board with 63 logged searches. A coordinator seeded the questions from five broadening searches, rewrote and reopened three questions closed as unclear, merged three duplicate sources, and checked 45 claims against their pages with a fetch tool. The checks corrected six quotes that the workers' fetch tool had paraphrased, and one claim whose quote did not support it. Every claim in section 1 is verified.
