# Responding to ambiguous leadership feedback

Research findings on feedback such as “do better,” with limited ability to obtain clarification.

**Scope:** Clarifying feedback, translating feedback into action, and handling continuing ambiguity. This report records the research results discussed on 5 November 2026. It separates experimental findings, observational associations, and authors’ practice recommendations.

## Findings at a glance

The retrieved research supports several components of a response. It does not establish a tested end-to-end method for resolving vague feedback when the leader cannot or will not clarify it. Dello Russo et al. (2023) reviewed 120 workplace studies and found limited practical guidance for feedback recipients beyond seeking feedback from supervisors and colleagues.

| Situation | Relevant finding | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One exchange remains possible | Requests for future advice produced more concrete developmental input than requests for feedback. | The studies assumed reviewer ability and willingness; they did not establish subsequent performance gains. |
| The leader is unavailable | Research identifies monitoring and inquiry through other sources as feedback-seeking methods. | Monitoring correlated with agreement between employee and supervisor ratings; this does not establish improved performance. |
| The desired outcome is known, but the method is not | Learning goals improved performance on a complex simulation. | Every group knew the success criterion. The experiment did not address an unknown criterion. |
| Performance information is available | Reflection combined with feedback improved performance more than feedback alone. | Reflection without feedback did not improve performance in the studied simulations. |
| Ambiguity persists | Feedback environments correlate with performance and burnout. Authors recommend third-party facilitation. | Correlations do not establish causation; facilitation recommendations do not establish an escalation threshold. |

Sources: Anseel et al. (2009, 2015), Blunden et al. (2026), Cannon and Witherspoon (2005), Katz et al. (2021), Seijts et al. (2004), and Williams and Johnson (2000).

## 1. Receptivity and accuracy constitute separate problems

Fulham et al. (2022) distinguish two requirements for effective feedback:

- **Receptivity:** The recipient remains willing to consider and act on feedback.
- **Discerning meaning and truth:** The recipient understands what the feedback means and assesses its accuracy.

Their review identifies barriers in the feedback, the giver, and the recipient. It assigns responsibility for removing those barriers to both parties. The distinction separates willingness to engage from confidence that a particular judgment is accurate or actionable.

Cannon and Witherspoon (2005) describe feedback that presents broad judgments about a person without the observations that support those judgments. Their examples come from executive-coaching work. The authors recommend identifying the behaviour, circumstances, and consequences behind the assessment.

## 2. A request for future advice can improve input concreteness

Blunden et al. (2026) compared requests for advice with requests for feedback. Their research included a field experiment with **27,432 comments** and two laboratory studies with **806 participants**.

Advice requests produced more concrete, actionable developmental input. The researchers linked this effect to the providers’ focus on the future. Independent raters judged concrete input more useful.

The authors report three limits relevant to restricted access to a leader:

1. The research assumed that reviewers could and would provide developmental input. The authors identify inability, lack of motivation, and symbolic feedback processes as possible limits on the intervention.
2. Recipients in the field study did not rate advice-condition input as more useful.
3. The research did not connect the comments to subsequent recipient behaviour or performance improvement.

The experiment supports a change in how input is requested. It does not establish that this change resolves an unwilling leader’s ambiguity or improves later appraisals.

For a concrete question, Cannon and Witherspoon (2005) propose:

> “Can you clarify what you would like to see me do differently?”

This question comes from a research-informed practice article. The authors did not compare its wording with alternatives in a controlled experiment.

## 3. Feedback seeking includes observation and multiple sources

Ashford et al. (2003) distinguish two feedback-seeking methods:

| Method | Activity | Required access |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inquiry | Ask another person for performance information. | A person who can respond. |
| Monitoring | Observe behaviour and events that can provide performance information. | Relevant workplace interactions or outcomes. |

Their review treats the source, timing, topic, and method of feedback seeking as separate choices. It considers everyday workplace interactions as well as formal reviews. The literature therefore includes routes beyond questioning the original feedback giver.

Williams and Johnson (2000) studied **125 employees**. Employees who reported more feedback monitoring showed greater agreement between their self-assessments and their supervisors’ performance ratings. The observational design does not establish that monitoring caused this agreement or improved performance.

Anseel et al. (2015) conducted a meta-analysis of feedback-seeking research. They found a **small relationship between feedback seeking and performance**. They also found that inquiry and monitoring were not interchangeable methods. Their findings support examining the specific method and outcome rather than assuming that seeking more feedback produces better performance.

## 4. Unknown methods and unknown success criteria require different evidence

Seijts et al. (2004) assigned **170 business students** to three goal conditions in a complex business simulation:

| Condition | Assigned goal |
| --- | --- |
| General goal | Do your best to increase market share. |
| Performance goal | Achieve at least 21% market share. |
| Learning goal | Identify and implement at least six strategies to increase market share. |

Participants with learning goals achieved higher performance than participants in either other condition. The performance-goal group did not outperform the general-goal group. The task required participants to discover effective strategies; effort and persistence alone did not suffice.

**Every group knew that market share defined success.** The experiment addressed how people pursue a known outcome when they need to discover a method. It did not address how employees should choose an outcome when a leader leaves the performance dimension unspecified.

The study therefore does not establish that converting “do better” into a self-selected numerical target resolves the leader’s expectation.

## 5. Reflection helped when it used feedback evidence

Anseel et al. (2009) tested a reflection intervention in two studies involving **640 and 488 participants**. Participants completed a work simulation and reflected on examples of their behaviour in response to feedback.

Reflection combined with feedback improved subsequent task performance more than feedback alone. Reflection without feedback did not improve performance in these studies.

The intervention connected performance information to identifiable behaviour. The study did not test whether prolonged interpretation of an unexplained phrase helps recipients reconstruct a leader’s unstated expectations. Its results concern a defined reflection activity and measured simulation performance.

## 6. Clearer feedback, improved work, and favourable evaluation are distinct outcomes

The retrieved studies measure different results:

| Outcome | Relevant study | What the study establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clearer developmental input | Blunden et al. (2026) | Advice requests increased comment concreteness. |
| Improved task performance | Seijts et al. (2004); Anseel et al. (2009) | Interventions improved performance on defined simulation tasks. |
| Agreement between employee and supervisor ratings | Williams and Johnson (2000) | Monitoring correlated with agreement between ratings. |
| Better later appraisal, promotion, or pay | Not established by these studies | The preceding findings do not demonstrate this outcome. |

Anseel and Sherf (2025) distinguish feedback about tasks with an identifiable correct solution from **judgmental feedback**, where preferences or social consensus define the criterion. Their review identifies divergent assumptions across feedback research and limits on the transfer of findings between settings.

For complex professional work, the retrieved evidence does not establish a procedure that guarantees both improved work outcomes and a favourable assessment from a leader whose criteria remain unknown.

## 7. Continuing ambiguity involves the feedback environment

Katz et al. (2021) combined **112 independent samples representing 31,089 workers**. A favourable feedback environment correlated with:

- Higher supervisor-rated performance: corrected correlation **r_c = .29**.
- Lower burnout: corrected correlation **r_c = −.51**.

These estimates concern the broader day-to-day feedback relationship. They do not isolate vague wording or establish that a particular recipient action causes better performance or lower burnout.

Cannon and Witherspoon (2005) recommend third-party involvement to help identify observations behind judgments, expose unclear statements, and facilitate discussion. They describe coaches, HR professionals, and other observers in this role. Their support comes from research-informed reasoning and cases, rather than a controlled comparison of escalation strategies.

The retrieved evidence did not establish when a recipient should continue adapting, seek mediation, change reporting relationships, or leave. It did not supply an escalation threshold based on a number of vague-feedback episodes.

## 8. Vague feedback does not identify the leader’s motive

Schaerer et al. (2018) examined performance appraisals and role-play interactions across six studies involving **1,883 participants**. Managers overestimated how well employees understood their feedback. This **illusion of transparency** was stronger for negative feedback.

The research demonstrates a mechanism through which managers can believe they communicated a message that employees did not receive. It does not establish that this mechanism explains a particular leader’s behaviour. The wording of an individual comment does not determine which mechanism produced it.

## Evidence gaps

| Open question | Status in the retrieved evidence |
| --- | --- |
| How should a recipient respond when the leader cannot or will not clarify? | Studies support components of a response; no tested complete method was identified. |
| How should a recipient choose an improvement target when the evaluation criterion is unknown? | Learning-goal experiments supplied the criterion and do not resolve this question. |
| Can another source substitute for the original leader? | Research identifies multiple sources and monitoring, but does not establish equivalent access to the leader’s criteria. |
| Which response improves both work outcomes and later appraisals? | The studies measure different outcomes and do not establish this combined result. |
| When should the recipient escalate or leave? | No evidence-based threshold was identified. |
| Do these findings validate an AI correction protocol? | The cited studies concern human feedback and work. They do not validate an AI protocol. |

These gaps describe the limits of the research retrieved for this inquiry. They do not establish that no relevant study exists.
