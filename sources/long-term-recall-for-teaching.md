# Long-term recall and returning to the material, for an AI agent that teaches one user

Research register · Search date: 10 November 2026

**Scope.** How a learner keeps what they learned in a one-to-one lesson over weeks to months, and how they return to it later. The register covers six topics: how far apart reviews should be, successive relearning, how to restart after a gap, whether learners follow review plans, whether to repeat or vary review questions, and how fast skills decay. It applies them to the `teach-better` skill's spaced practice (section 4.2) and take-away (section 6.2). It extends `sources/take-away-after-lesson-for-teaching.md` and does not repeat it.

**Status.** 9 questions closed, all answered, several on thin evidence. The coordinator checked 43 quotes against their sources. Most evidence covers facts, vocabulary and concepts, from lab and course studies, with tests at most one year later. Many sources were read as abstracts. No study tested review that an AI tutor scheduled over months, followed by a test without AI.

The best gap between reviews depends on how long the learner must remember the material. In a study of more than 1,350 people, the best gap was about 20 to 40% of a one-week test delay and about 5 to 10% of a one-year delay. A gap that was too long cost much less than one that was too short (Cepeda et al., 2008). Recalling material again in later sessions gave large gains over a single session (Rawson et al., 2018). The recommended schedule is 3 correct recalls first, then 3 relearning sessions spaced widely apart (Rawson & Dunlosky, 2011). Learners rarely schedule review themselves (Hartwig & Dunlosky, 2012).

## 1. Evidence, with how strong it is

### 1.1. The gap between reviews

**Evidence: one large study plus lab studies; read in full for Cepeda et al. (2008).**

- The best gap "increased as test delay increased. However, when measured as a proportion of test delay, the optimal gap declined from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay" (Cepeda et al., 2008).
- "Although there are costs to using a gap that is longer than the optimal value, these costs are much smaller than the costs of using too short a gap" (Cepeda et al., 2008).
- "If a person wishes to retain information for several years, a delayed review of at least several months seems likely to produce a highly favorable return on the time investment" (Cepeda et al., 2008).
- Thirteen sessions spaced 56 days apart gave retention comparable to 26 sessions spaced 14 days apart. The study had four participants (Bahrick et al., 1993).

### 1.2. Expanding or equal gaps

**Evidence: consistent; mostly abstracts.**

- Expanding gaps gave recall "equivalent to that from equal-interval practice on a final test given eight weeks after training". Expanding gaps kept recall higher during the training period (Kang et al., 2014, abstract only).
- "No evidence was found for differences between expanding and equal spacing" (Mettler et al., 2016). In the same study, scheduling adapted to each learner's answers beat fixed schedules.
- "The important factor for promoting long-term retention is delaying initial retrieval to make it more difficult" (Karpicke & Roediger, 2007, abstract only).

### 1.3. Successive relearning

**Evidence: one lab group; read as abstracts.**

- Successive relearning means recalling items to a criterion in one session, then recalling them again in later spaced sessions (Rawson & Dunlosky, 2011).
- "Our prescriptive conclusion for students is to practice recalling concepts to an initial criterion of 3 correct recalls and then to relearn them 3 times at widely spaced intervals" (Rawson & Dunlosky, 2011).
- The advantage of successive relearning over learning in a single session was "substantial, ds = 1.52 to 4.19" (Rawson et al., 2018).
- Once relearning sessions follow, learning to a higher criterion in the first session is "not efficient" (Vaughn et al., 2016).

### 1.4. Returning after a gap: recall first, then restudy

**Evidence: consistent but indirect; delays of at most a week.**

- Restudying beat testing at 5 minutes, but testing won on delayed tests (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
- "Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance the effectiveness of subsequent restudy" (Arnold & McDermott, 2013, abstract only). This held for meaningful questions, even when study came up to 24 hours later (Kornell, 2014, abstract only).
- "Repeated testing during initial learning is also a very effective strategy to enhance delayed relearning" (de Jonge et al., 2014).
- No study tested adults returning to lesson material after weeks or months.

### 1.5. Whether learners follow a review plan

**Evidence: thin; mostly medical learners.**

- "Relatively few students overall (13%) schedule their study ahead of time" (Hartwig & Dunlosky, 2012).
- "The daily mass text messages did not successfully induce residents to answer practice questions" (Nelson et al., 2025).
- In programs where a system scheduled the review and learners chose to enrol, 74% completed the program (Shaw et al., 2011, abstract only). The median completion score in another was 98% (Kerfoot & Baker, 2012, abstract only). Counting only people who enrolled likely overstates adherence.
- Students completed over 90% of relearning sessions that earned course credit and under 10% of optional ones (Greve et al., 2023, preprint, abstract only).
- Review scheduled for each student by a model gave a 16.5% boost in course retention over massed study (Lindsey et al., 2014, abstract only).
- No study tested calendar entries, implementation intentions or reminders for review of lesson material.

### 1.6. Repeating or varying review questions

**Evidence: lab studies, delays of at most a week.**

- Testing transferred to new questions on average, d = 0.40. Transfer was often absent when the moderators the meta-analysis identified were missing (Pan & Rickard, 2018, abstract only).
- "Repeated testing produced superior retention and transfer on the final test relative to repeated studying" (Butler, 2010, abstract only).
- Variable cues beat constant cues for learning foreign words. Learners "judged learning to be more effective with constant rather than variable cues" (Butowska-Buczynska et al., 2024, abstract only).
- Varied practice on anagrams beat repeated practice, even on the anagram that had been repeated (Goode et al., 2008, abstract only).

### 1.7. Feedback during review

**Evidence: one meta-analysis plus experiments over days.**

- Testing effects were larger with feedback, g = 0.73, than without it, g = 0.39 (Rowland, 2014).
- Without feedback, initial test performance at or below 50% "did not produce a reliable testing effect" (Rowland, 2014).
- Both immediate and delayed feedback reduced the learning of wrong answers. Delayed feedback gave a little more (Butler & Roediger, 2008; the coordinator could not reopen this source to recheck the quotes).

### 1.8. How fast skills decay

**Evidence: two meta-analyses of trained skills.**

- Skill loss ranged from d = −0.01 right after training to d = −1.4 after more than 365 days without use. "Physical, natural, and speed-based tasks were less susceptible to skill loss than cognitive, artificial, and accuracy-based tasks" (Arthur et al., 1998).
- "With lack of use, half of initial skill acquisition performance gains were lost after approximately 6.5 months for accuracy, 13 months for speed, and 11 months for mixed performance" (Tatel & Ackerman, 2025, abstract only).
- Brief, frequent refresher training kept CPR skills: retention was 2.3 times more likely after 2 trainings and 2.9 times after 3 (Sutton et al., 2011, abstract only).
- Both meta-analyses cover trained skills, not recall of knowledge from a lesson.

### 1.9. Spaced-repetition apps and AI

**Evidence: medical learners; thin for AI.**

- In a randomized study of 26,258 physicians, spaced repetition beat no spaced repetition for learning (d = 0.62), with a smaller gain on rewritten questions (Price et al., 2025, abstract only).
- A meta-analysis of studies in medical education found an effect in favour of spaced repetition (Maye & Hurley, 2026, abstract only). Evidence on Anki itself "is largely observational" (Frappa et al., 2026, abstract only).
- In a survey with an 18% response rate, 66% of medical students who used Anki reviewed daily (Wothe et al., 2023).
- In a 7-week study of 89 students, an LLM agent that asked spaced retrieval questions and adapted them gave higher end-of-semester scores than open-ended AI study, d = 0.92. The number and timing of sessions were the same in every condition (Akgun & Toker, 2026, preprint).

## 2. Where the evidence is weak or disagrees

- **Delays.** The evidence on returning after a gap, on varying questions and on feedback covers at most a week. Only the spacing studies reach a year.
- **Materials.** Facts, vocabulary and concepts dominate. Skill-decay meta-analyses cover procedural skills, not knowledge from a lesson.
- **Adherence.** Completion figures come from people who chose to enrol, mostly medical learners.
- **Expanding gaps.** Expanding gaps kept recall higher during training in one study, with no difference on the final test.

## 3. What this means for `teach-better`

The user later asked to optimise the skill for using the topic, not for recalling it. `sources/transfer-to-use-for-teaching.md` revises these points: practice and review use tasks from the user's own situations, and the take-away is a reference for use.

These are inferences from the evidence above, not tested rules.

1. **Ask how long the user needs to remember the topic.** The best gap depends on it.
2. **Set the first gap from that period.** Use about a fifth to a third of it for a week or a few weeks, and about a twentieth to a tenth for a year. When unsure, choose the longer gap.
3. **Plan three spaced review sessions.** In each, the user answers until each question is right once. This matches successive relearning.
4. **Keep later gaps equal or growing.** Neither beat the other on later tests.
5. **Start a return with recall, not rereading.** Failed attempts still help the restudy that follows.
6. **Give feedback, and re-teach when most answers fail.** Recall that mostly fails, with no feedback, gave no reliable benefit.
7. **Vary the questions when the agent runs the review.** Varied cues beat constant ones in lab studies, and learners judged them less helpful.
8. **Offer to schedule the reviews, with permission.** Learners rarely schedule review themselves, and reminder messages failed. No study tested calendar entries.
9. **Suggest a later refresher for skills the user will not use.** Unused skills lost about half their accuracy gains in about six months.

## 4. Method

- Research board: `.research/long-term-recall/` (not committed). It holds every question, claim and source, with each source marked by how it was read.
- Workers on a cheaper model researched the questions. Broadening searches included one in German. The coordinator checked 43 quotes against their sources, through PubMed and Europe PMC abstracts, full texts where available, and a saved PDF of Cepeda et al. (2008).
- A worker had read an in-press manuscript of Cepeda et al. (2008) whose figures differ from the published version. This report uses the published figures.

## 5. Open questions

- **Returning after weeks or months** to lesson material, recall first against reread first. None found.
- **Calendar entries, reminders or implementation intentions** for review of lesson material. None found.
- **Identical against varied review questions** over weeks to months. None found.
- **Review scheduled by an AI tutor** over months, with a test taken without AI. None found. Barcaui (2025), a trial reporting lower retention at 45 days for ChatGPT users, was read only through a news report.
- **Drop-out from spaced-repetition apps** among adults learning alone. None found.
