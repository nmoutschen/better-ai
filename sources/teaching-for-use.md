# Teaching for lifelong use, not recall

Research register · Search date: 11 November 2026

**Scope.** Evidence to redesign the `teach-better` skill around its intended outcome: adults use a topic in their own life and work, with understanding, over time. The register covers:
- the learner's own situations and authentic tasks
- conceptual understanding and flexible use
- checking understanding without closed-book tests
- usable written references
- sequencing a larger plan
- learning from real use
- epistemic failures in AI-assembled content
- how well easy-to-collect measures predict use

Earlier registers already cover predictors of use, practice that resembles the real task, plans, a reference at hand and feedback on real use (`transfer-to-use-for-teaching.md`). They also cover inert knowledge and near misses (`applicability-for-teaching.md`), and self-study material (`static-self-study-materials.md`).

**Status.** 8 questions answered, most on thin evidence. One low-priority question on terms stayed open. 45 of 50 claims were checked against their source. Many key primary papers were blocked, among them Alliger et al. (1997), Sitzmann et al. (2008) and Blume et al. (2010). Most experimental evidence comes from school mathematics, software manuals and workplace training, measured by tests rather than later use.

Four findings shape the rewrite:
- **Measures:** the measures that are easy to collect predict little about later use. Reactions show "very little reason to believe" they track learning or behaviour, and effect sizes "decreased substantially" from learning criteria to behaviour at work (Arthur et al., 2003; Grossman & Salas, 2011). This supports the user's point that research favours what is measurable.
- **The learner's own situations:** this is not tested directly. No study compares an adult's own-work example with a generic one on later use. Related evidence is mixed: personalising to interests helped only when its depth matched the learner (Walkington & Bernacki, 2018).
- **Guidance:** it matters more than authenticity. Unassisted discovery did worse than explicit instruction, and guided discovery did better (Alfieri et al., 2011). Whole tasks ordered from simple to complex had a large but variable effect (Costa et al., 2022).
- **Grounding:** AI-assembled content fails in three checked ways. It fabricates citations, attaches real citations that do not support the claim, and passes on retracted or wrong research silently (Walters & Wilder, 2023; Liu et al., 2023; University of Sheffield, 2025).

## 1. What predicts use, and what measures miss it

**Evidence: one meta-analysis read in full; the primary studies it cites were blocked.**

- Reactions: "there is very little reason to believe that how trainees feel about or whether they like a training program tells researchers much, if anything, about (a) how much they learned from the program ..., (b) changes in their job-related behavior" (Arthur et al., 2003). The criteria have "weak intercorrelations" (Alliger et al., 1997, as cited by Arthur et al., 2003).
- "When learning criteria were compared with subsequent behavioral criteria (that is, work behaviors), effect sizes decreased substantially" (Grossman & Salas, 2011, citing Arthur et al., 2003).
- In practice, "78% of the benchmarking organizations surveyed reported using reaction measures, compared with 32%, 9%, and 7% for learning, behavioral, and results". The reason given is that reactions are easy to collect and close at hand (Arthur et al., 2003).
- Reactions are "only modestly related to immediate knowledge outcomes and unrelated to delayed knowledge". Only utility reactions, whether the training seems useful, have "a modest connection" to transfer (QIC-WD, 2022, summarising Sitzmann et al., 2008, and Blume et al., 2010).

## 2. The learner's own situations and authentic tasks

**Evidence: two meta-analyses read in full; personalisation studies read as abstracts; no direct test.**

- Whole-task programmes (4C/ID) had "a high impact on performance (d = 0.79)", with "a high degree of heterogeneity (I2 = 78.6%)". The effect was stronger in higher education (Costa et al., 2022). In one randomised study with 65 adult programming novices, whole tasks beat part tasks on near and far transfer (Rosenberg-Kima et al., 2022, abstract).
- Problems personalised to students' interests raised interest and classroom exam performance in algebra (Bernacki & Walkington, 2018, abstract). But "benefits may only be realized when students' degree of quantitative engagement with their out-of-school interests matches the depth at which the personalized problems are written" (Walkington & Bernacki, 2018, abstract). Earlier work found that personalised vocabulary examples raised motivation without improving learning (see `ai-agent-teaching-new-topics.md`).
- "Unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations do" (Alfieri et al., 2011).
- No study compared first problems from an adult's own work with generic problems, measured by later use. This matches the earlier register (`applicability-for-teaching.md`). The user's recollection, that the research shows no benefit, is closer to "untested" than to "tested and failed".

## 3. Understanding and flexible use

**Evidence: three studies read in full, all of children learning mathematics.**

- Conceptual and procedural knowledge build each other: "children's initial conceptual knowledge predicted gains in procedural knowledge, and gains in procedural knowledge predicted improvements in conceptual knowledge". Prior conceptual knowledge was "more strongly related to transfer of procedures to novel problems" than to the practised problems (Rittle-Johnson et al., 2001).
- "Both kinds of knowledge contributed independently to procedural flexibility" (Schneider et al., 2011).
- Comparing solution methods raised flexibility and procedural knowledge, not conceptual knowledge. The conceptual gains came only for students with some prior knowledge. In a classroom trial, comparison materials had no overall effect, and teachers used them little (Rittle-Johnson et al., 2017).
- No adult or workplace evidence was found.

## 4. Checking understanding without closed-book tests

**Evidence: three meta-analyses read in full; none tests later use.**

- Practice tests transfer to new questions (d 0.40), but after correction the effect often disappears unless retrieval is elaborated or matches the later task (Pan & Rickard, 2018).
- Self-explanation prompts: g = .55 overall, with transfer (g = .53) no larger than recall (g = .50) (Bisra et al., 2018).
- Explaining to someone face to face beat preparing to teach without doing it (g = 0.84 against 0.48) (Kobayashi, 2019).
- Teach-back in chronic-disease education improved knowledge in four studies (Dinh et al., 2016, abstract).
- No source tested whether explaining, applying to a new case, or an open-book task predicts later use.

## 5. Usable references

**Evidence: thin; mostly 1990s software manuals.**

- Minimal manuals: "minimal manual users completed about 50% more tasks successfully on a performance test and displayed significantly more self-reliance". Training time did not differ significantly (227 against 261 minutes), and the sample was 25 people (van der Meij & Lazonder, 1993). Critics say the effect is "hopelessly confounded" with other variables (Williams & Farkas, 1992).
- People rarely read manuals and struggle to find search terms (Novick & Ward, 2006, abstract).
- Developers had productivity problems "when documentation lacks usage examples" (Sohan et al., 2017).
- No controlled test of consistent structure, headings or signalling for lookup was found.

## 6. Sequencing a larger plan

**Evidence: thin.**

- 4C/ID orders whole tasks into classes "deliberately sequenced from simple-to-complex" (Daniel et al., 2018, a design paper with no outcomes).
- In self-paced distance courses, there was a "significant negative correlation between students' sequential movement through the course and successful completion". The author recommends learner control (Lim, 2016; correlational).

## 7. Learning from real use

**Evidence: two meta-analyses, one read in full.**

- "Debriefs improve effectiveness over a control group by approximately 25% (d = .67)", with "a bolstering effect of alignment" to the task (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli, 2013).
- Feedback helps on average (d = .41), but "over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance" (Kluger & DeNisi, 1996, abstract).
- No source covered personal notes, or how a reference supports learning from use.

## 8. Epistemic failures in AI-assembled content

**Evidence: studies read as abstracts or news; figures are for 2023–2025 models.**

- "55% of the GPT-3.5 citations but just 18% of the GPT-4 citations are fabricated", and "43% of the real (non-fabricated) GPT-3.5 citations but just 24% of the real GPT-4 citations include substantive citation errors" (Walters & Wilder, 2023).
- In generative search engines, "a mere 51.5% of generated sentences are fully supported by citations and only 74.5% of citations support their associated sentence" (Liu et al., 2023).
- "None of the 6,510 reports which ChatGPT produced mentioned that the articles were retracted or had relevant errors" (University of Sheffield, 2025). ChatGPT-5.0 "utilized 61.5% (72/117) of retracted articles" in question answering (Zhang et al., 2025).
- The practice that catches these: check each reference against bibliographic databases and read the cited source for the claim. No independent evaluation of checker tools was read.

## 9. Open questions and gaps

- Does building on the learner's own situations improve later use for adults? Untested.
- Does any check of understanding (explanation, application, open book) predict later use? Untested.
- Does a consistent, predictable reference structure help lookup? No controlled test found.
- Primary papers not read: Alliger et al. (1997), Sitzmann et al. (2008), Blume et al. (2010), Lim (2009), Sinha and Kapur (2021).
- What terms sources use for teaching aimed at use (low priority, not researched).
