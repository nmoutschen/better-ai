# Manager and individual contributor relationships: what the IC can do, and what transfers to a user directing an AI agent

Research findings for the better-ai skills  
Prepared for Nicolas · Search date: 8 November 2026

**The manager does most of the work that sets the quality of the relationship. The individual contributor (IC) has a smaller lever, and how well it works depends on how the manager reads the IC's motive.** In a meta-analysis of 247 studies, factors on the leader's side explained the most variance in relationship quality (Dulebohn et al., 2012). On the IC's side, the evidence supports three behaviours: seeking feedback for the sake of the work, raising problems and suggestions at a moderate frequency, and performing reliably. Image management, repeated apologies and self-promotion tend to backfire. For a user directing an AI agent, only blogs and four small user studies were found. They point to the same levers: express uncertainty openly, raise problems instead of agreeing, and ask questions only when they are relevant. No study tests the comparison between managing a person and directing an agent.

**Scope.** This report covers what an IC (an employee with no reports of their own) can do to improve the relationship with their manager, and how those findings might transfer to an AI agent working for a user, with the user in the manager's role. `sources/ambiguous-leadership-feedback-research-results.md` covers how to respond to vague feedback, and this report does not repeat it.

**Status.** The research stopped at the `medium` level: no high- or medium-priority question is still open. One low-priority question is open (section 8). I checked the quotes of 56 of the board's 63 claims against their pages. Six claims could not be checked because their pages refused automated reading (Emerald journals and a Durham thesis). One claim was rejected because its quote did not support it. The research board is in `.research/manager-ic-relationship/`.

**Reading level of the evidence.** Most academic sources were read as abstracts, because the full texts are paywalled. Exceptions read in full: Huang et al. (2018), Peck and Levashina (2017), Bastardoz and Adriaensen (2023), and the doctoral thesis behind Lam et al. (2007). Every source on the user–AI comparison is a blog except four user studies (section 6).

## 1. Findings at a glance

| IC behaviour | Finding | Condition | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Seeking feedback** | Linked to a better relationship and better objective performance in 499 manager–report pairs in China (Lam et al., 2007) | **Only when the manager attributes it to improving performance, not to managing impressions** | One strong study; abstract read, thesis read in full |
| **Raising problems and suggestions (voice)** | Managers rate a moderate frequency higher than speaking up rarely or very often (Huang et al., 2018) | Very frequent problem-raising draws negative reactions; relationship quality moderates the effect | Full text read |
| **Speaking up and taking charge** | Linked to a better relationship through the leader seeing the employee as constructive (Shih & Nguyen, 2023) | Stronger for employees who already perform well | Abstract only, two Taiwanese samples |
| **Communicating often** | Helps performance ratings when the relationship is good, and hurts them when it is poor (Kacmar et al., 2003) | Depends on existing relationship quality | Two samples (N = 188 and N = 153) |
| **Performing and helping colleagues** | Raises the supervisor's trust in the subordinate (*The effects of subordinate's job performance…*, 2009) | Helping counts only when the supervisor sees it as genuine | One Korean survey |
| **Persuading with facts** | Rational persuasion relates positively to outcomes and pressure relates negatively (Lee et al., 2017) | Not separated for influence aimed upward | Meta-analysis, abstract only |
| **Repeated apologies** | Lowered performance ratings more with each repetition (Bolino et al., 2014) | Stronger early in the relationship | Lab study, abstract only |
| **Self-promotion** | Can be disbelieved and "risks arrogance" (Peck & Levashina, 2017) | Worse when claims differ from what the supervisor sees | Meta-analysis, full text |

## 2. Terms

- **Managing up**: practitioners' term for "the process of consciously working with your superior to obtain the best possible results for you, your boss, and the company". Its authors exclude "political maneuvering or … apple polishing" (Gabarro & Kotter, 1980/2005). Other practitioners describe it as a partnership (Boogaard, 2025; Osmani, 2024).
- **Leader-member exchange (LMX)**: researchers' measure of how good the relationship is between one manager and one report. A high-quality relationship is "characterized by a high degree of mutual trust, respect, and obligation". The manager has more influence over it, but "the follower also plays an important part in creating the relationship" (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.).
- **Upward influence**: "the ability to influence your boss and others in positions higher than yours", through tactics such as rational persuasion, ingratiation and coalition building (*The power to influence*, n.d.).
- **Followership**: role-based research treats followers as people with low formal power, such as direct reports. Process-based research treats leading and following as one social influence process. Bastardoz and Adriaensen (2023) argue that a report's upward leading and the manager's following "jointly and simultaneously emerge".
- **Voice**: speaking up to the manager. Promotive voice suggests improvements; prohibitive voice points out problems (Huang et al., 2018).

## 3. What the IC can do

### 3.1. Seek feedback, for the work

In two studies with 499 manager–report pairs in China, feedback seeking predicted a better relationship and better objective performance "only when supervisors interpreted the feedback-seeking behavior as being driven more by performance enhancement motives and less by impression management motives" (Lam et al., 2007). The thesis behind that paper reports a small direct effect (β = .14) and a larger effect when supervisors saw weak impression-management motives (simple slope .41). Korean data link a good relationship with preferring direct feedback seeking, but the study is cross-sectional, so the direction is unclear (Lee et al., 2007).

### 3.2. Raise problems, at a moderate rate

Managers "give more positive evaluations to employees who engage in a moderate frequency of promotive/prohibitive voice than those who either rarely speak up or speak up very frequently" (Huang et al., 2018). The authors conclude that "a moderate frequency of prohibitive voice can project a positive image to managers". Frequency matters in a second way: frequent communication improved ratings when the relationship was good and lowered them when it was poor (Kacmar et al., 2003).

### 3.3. Perform reliably and report problems early

Research on what makes a supervisor trust a subordinate is thin. A review of 73 trust studies found "a dearth of attention on subordinate attributes" (Nienaber et al., 2015; unverified). A Korean survey found supervisors trust high performers. When performance was low, helping colleagues raised trust, and only when the supervisor saw the help as altruistic (*The effects of subordinate's job performance…*, 2009). A doctoral thesis found ability, benevolence and integrity predicted a leader's trust more strongly than availability and receptivity (Zheng, 2018; unverified).

Practitioner sources add advice that research has not tested:

- Report problems before the manager finds out: "Nothing erodes trust faster than your manager learning about a problem from someone else, or from the outcome directly", and "Surprised by your own mistake is forgivable. Caught is not" (Buteau, 2026b).
- Be reliable: "your boss can count on you to take on a task and see it through to the finish line". Otherwise "they'll have no choice but to micromanage you" (*Build trust*, 2023).
- Keep status updates short, "a few minutes, max", with "just a handful of key points" (Kaplan-Moss, 2021).
- Bring proposed solutions together with problems (Jedrzejewski, 2024).

## 4. What backfires

- **Repeated apologies** "had an increasingly negative effect on evaluations of performance", while repeated ingratiation raised them (Bolino et al., 2014).
- **Self-promotion** "may even be unbelievable if such claims differ from the supervisor's own perceptions", and repeating it "risks arrogance" (Peck & Levashina, 2017).
- **Ingratiation early in a relationship**: a supervisor's early perception that a newcomer was ingratiating "generally had negative effect on initial LMX" (Wang & Liu, 2020, conference abstract).
- **Ingratiation in general** has "a small positive effect … on performance evaluations" (Gordon, 1996). Other-focused tactics such as ingratiation relate to performance ratings (rc = 0.25), while self-promotion does not significantly (Peck & Levashina, 2017).

The common thread is perceived motive. The same behaviour helps when the manager reads it as serving the work and hurts when they read it as serving the IC's image.

## 5. Where sources disagree

**Who is responsible for the relationship.** Practitioner sources place the work on the IC (Gabarro & Kotter, 1980/2005; Osmani, 2024; Buteau, 2026a). Research places most of it on the manager:

- Leader variables explained the most variance in relationship quality (Dulebohn et al., 2012).
- Employees speak up more when the manager signals "openness, support, humility, ethical conduct, responsiveness, and legitimacy for upward communication" (Edon et al., 2026; not yet peer reviewed).
- In remote work, leaders made more effort than their reports to keep feedback flowing, and the authors call for "a more proactive role from subordinates" (Jansson & Kangas, 2024; unverified).

**Where managing up ends and politics begins.** Practitioners draw the line by honesty: managing up "is not telling your manager what they want to hear. It's not withholding bad news to protect the relationship" (Buteau, 2026a). Research shows ingratiation does work a little (Gordon, 1996), and draws the line by the motive the manager perceives, not by effectiveness.

## 6. The user as manager, the AI agent as IC

### 6.1. Where the comparison holds and where it breaks

Practitioner essays agree that the logic of delegation carries over to AI agents: a goal, granted authority, monitoring, trust extended step by step, and accountability that stays with the human. They say it breaks in three places:

- **No social contract.** "The social contract that keeps human delegation honest doesn't exist in human-AI delegation", because an agent has no reputation or career at stake (Koh, 2026).
- **No objection.** An agent is "often unlikely to hesitate, object", and its "sycophantic agreement" is harder to spot than a person's evasion or inexperience (Spinellis, 2026).
- **No memory, and different speed.** Agents do not remember between sessions and work faster than people (Spinellis, 2026). One vendor argues that agents given persistent context should get ownership with check-ins and escalation thresholds rather than intern-style hand-holding (Tom W. & the Scout A. Team, 2026).

All of these sources are blogs. No empirical study of the comparison was found.

### 6.2. User studies on agent behaviour

- **Expressing uncertainty.** When a language model said "I'm not sure, but…", users' confidence in it and agreement with it dropped, while their accuracy rose. Uncertainty phrased from a general perspective ("It's not clear, but…") had weaker, non-significant effects (Kim et al., 2024; N = 404, pre-registered).
- **Control at points of uncertainty.** Users of a workflow agent became more willing to delegate, but "still required control at points of uncertainty" (Long et al., 2025).
- **Clarifying questions.** Users valued questions that were "thought-provoking, open-ended, or offered unique insights" (Tix, 2024). In a search study, "a fraction of them stops due to fatigue or due to receiving irrelevant questions" (Zou et al., 2020).

### 6.3. Inferences for agent behaviour

These are inferences from sections 3 to 6.2. No source tests them in the user–agent setting.

| Manager–IC finding | Possible agent behaviour |
|---|---|
| Feedback seeking helps only when read as serving the work | Ask questions that visibly serve the task; avoid questions that only shift responsibility to the user |
| Moderate voice is rated best; agents tend not to object | Raise problems and disagree when warranted, but not on every turn |
| Hiding mistakes erodes trust (practitioner) | Report problems and own mistakes before the user discovers them |
| Repeated apologies lower ratings | Correct the work instead of apologising repeatedly |
| Self-promotion is disbelieved when it differs from what the manager sees | Report results the user can check, without overstating them |
| First-person uncertainty lowers trust but raises accuracy | State uncertainty in the first person when it exists, accepting lower confidence |

## 7. Contexts covered

- **Culture.** Upward influence strategies vary partly with cultural values across six countries (Ralston et al., 2001). In Taiwan, a supervisor's power-distance orientation changed how upward strategies affected trust and delegation (Cheng, 2016, master's thesis).
- **Remote work.** One Finnish interview study (Jansson & Kangas, 2024; unverified).
- **Newcomers.** One conference abstract (Wang & Liu, 2020).
- **Profession, including software roles.** No readable study was found.

## 8. Gaps and open questions

- The Dulebohn et al. (2012) effect sizes for specific follower behaviours could not be read.
- The methods behind Gabarro and Kotter's framework were not found.
- No study compares manager–IC behaviours across professions or in software teams.
- No study tests how users interpret an agent's motive when it asks questions, the counterpart of the feedback-seeking finding.
- Six claims are unverified because their pages blocked automated reading: Nienaber et al. (2015), Knoll and Gill (2011), Scrimpshire et al. (2021), Zheng (2018), and both claims from Jansson and Kangas (2024).
- Open, low priority: what meta-analyses of voice show about promotive compared with prohibitive voice.
