# Writing for human cognition: Text design and cognition during AI-assisted work

Literature review and research register  
Prepared for Nicolas · Search date: 4 November 2026

**Scope.** This review covers text design for readers and cognition during AI-assisted work. It examines comprehension, recall, transfer, information search, judgment of correctness, reliance on AI, and learning after assistance ends. It excludes writing as a means of developing the writer’s own ideas, except where a reader’s response forms part of an investigated learning activity.

**Status.** This document presents a source-led research synthesis for a master’s thesis. It does not constitute a completed empirical thesis or an exhaustive systematic review. The search produced answers to bounded questions and left six questions open. The requested empty-register stopping condition has therefore not been met. The register preserves those questions rather than converting missing evidence into conclusions.

**Reading route.** Sections 1–5 report the literature. Section 6 specifies how this report applies it. Section 7 proposes an evaluation protocol. Sections 8–9 document search decisions, evidence access, and remaining questions. Section 10 supplies APA references.

## 1. Define the outcome before choosing the writing rule

The studies in this review measure different outcomes. They sometimes report gains in one outcome without gains in another. A comprehension result cannot establish a memory benefit; a confidence result cannot establish better judgment; an assisted task score cannot establish retained learning. The heading experiment, explanation-calibration experiments, and classroom AI trial provide examples of these separations (Prinz-Weiß & König, 2023; Steyvers et al., 2025; Bastani et al., 2025).

| Outcome | What a test asks the reader to do | What the result does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Comprehension | Answer questions about relations, implications, or meaning | Retention after a delay |
| Recall | Reproduce information without the source | Ability to apply the information to a new case |
| Transfer | Solve a case that differs from the learning material | Transfer to an unrelated domain |
| Information search | Locate relevant material | Understanding of the whole document |
| Judgment calibration | Match confidence to correctness | High accuracy on its own |
| Discernment | Distinguish correct from incorrect claims | Willingness to verify sources |
| Assisted performance | Complete a task with AI available | Ability to complete it after AI removal |
| Unaided learning | Complete a later task without AI | Durable workplace expertise |
| Perceived ease or quality | Rate clarity, helpfulness, confidence, or effort | Objective comprehension |

The table defines the outcomes used in this report. It does not claim that one instrument measures each outcome in all settings.

Kintsch’s (1988) construction–integration account distinguishes linguistic form, a propositional representation of the text, and a situation model that incorporates knowledge. The account provides a theoretical explanation for why recognition of wording and understanding of a described situation can diverge. It does not prescribe a universal sentence length or document template.

Cowan’s (2001) review concerns a capacity of about three to five chunks under conditions that constrain rehearsal and recoding. A chunk depends on the task and the reader’s knowledge. The paper does not establish a maximum of four bullets, four headings, or four concepts per answer. Translating the laboratory estimate into a formatting limit would require a separate test.

## 2. What text design changes

### 2.1 Wording and syntax

Martínez et al. (2022) combined a corpus analysis with two experiments, with 184 participants across the experiments. Participants showed poorer comprehension and recall for legal-register text than for simplified versions. Center embedding and low-frequency wording contributed more to recall difficulty than passive constructions did. The experiments concern legal language; they do not establish the same effect sizes for engineering documents.

Paolazzi et al. (2019) provide a qualification for a universal active-voice rule. Across four experiments, participants read passive sentences faster, yet made more errors on questions about who acted on whom. Processing time and comprehension accuracy therefore yielded different comparisons. An instruction to “use active voice” can express a style preference without becoming a claim that every passive sentence imposes greater processing cost.

The reporting choice here is to name actors and preserve the relations between them. This report uses active voice as requested. It does not count compliance with that preference as a measured cognitive benefit.

### 2.2 Coherence and prior knowledge

McNamara et al. (1996) manipulated text coherence and examined readers with different background knowledge. Greater coherence helped readers with less knowledge; under some conditions, readers with more knowledge benefited from the inferential work that less coherent text required. The measured task and level of understanding affected the result.

Gilabert et al. (2005) tested two revision approaches in two experiments. A version designed to support inferential activity benefited both higher- and lower-knowledge readers on recall and inference measures. A version designed to reduce that activity benefited recall but not inference. These findings qualify the claim that experts benefit from omitted connections: the revision procedure matters.

**Consequence for the thesis.** “More explanation” and “less explanation” do not define a sufficient experimental contrast. A comparison must identify which causal links, definitions, examples, or inferential steps the revision changes, and which knowledge readers bring. This is a proposed specification for the research design, not a new result.

### 2.3 Headings and signals

Hyönä and Lorch (2004) used eye tracking to examine topic headings. Headings facilitated processing of sentences that introduced topics; participants also included more topics in summaries. The findings concern organization and topic processing.

Prinz-Weiß and König (2023) assigned 51 university students to texts with or without headings. Headings improved comprehension and the accuracy of judgments about comprehension. The study did not detect significant effects on memory or judgments about memory. The texts contained about 500 words each. This is evidence for a defined intervention and population, not a universal heading-depth rule.

Schneider et al. (2018) synthesized 103 studies with 12,201 participants. Signals in learning media yielded pooled effects of g = 0.53 for retention and g = 0.33 for transfer. Their category includes several forms of signaling. Those estimates cannot be assigned to headings alone or treated as percentages.

| Evidence | Intervention | Outcome supported by the cited result | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyönä and Lorch (2004) | Topic headings | Topic processing and topic inclusion in summaries | Adult reading task |
| Prinz-Weiß and König (2023) | Headings in short science texts | Comprehension and comprehension judgments | Small university sample; no detected memory effect |
| Schneider et al. (2018) | Signals across learning media | Pooled retention and transfer gains | Several signal types and media |

### 2.4 Lists and selective emphasis

Jansen (2014) reported five studies of functional texts, including letters, flyers, and recipes. Bulleted presentation improved recall of listed material in three studies. Two studies found poorer recall of surrounding material. Lists of heterogeneous items showed weaker benefits.

This evidence supports a question about the distribution of attention and memory, rather than a general claim that lists improve the whole document. In an evaluation of a revised answer, the test should cover both highlighted claims and their qualifications. Otherwise, a gain on selected claims could conceal a loss elsewhere. The test recommendation is an application of the reported tradeoff.

### 2.5 Graphics and spatial integration

Guo et al. (2020) synthesized 39 experimental studies with 2,103 participants. Graphics had a pooled positive effect on reading comprehension, g = 0.39, 95% CI [0.26, 0.51]. Effects varied across studies. The review did not establish that each graphic type or every added illustration improves learning.

Schroeder and Cenkci (2018) synthesized 58 independent comparisons involving 2,426 participants. Spatial integration of related text and graphics yielded a pooled effect of g = 0.63. Their 2020 review asked a different question: whether cognitive-load measures supported the proposed split-attention mechanism. Across 41 comparisons, those measures did not provide consistent support for that explanation (Schroeder & Cenkci, 2020).

The learning effect and the proposed mechanism therefore require separate statements. A positive test score does not, by itself, demonstrate that a design lowered extraneous cognitive load.

Castro-Alonso et al. (2021) reviewed multimedia, contiguity, redundancy, signaling, and segmentation strategies. Their review records boundary conditions and null replications. Much segmentation evidence concerns instruction with time-controlled presentation, such as video. It does not establish an optimal paragraph size for self-paced reading.

### 2.6 Reader differences and accessibility

Galliussi et al. (2020) studied 128 Italian children, half with dyslexia. Dyslexia-oriented letterforms did not improve the measured reading outcomes. Increasing letter spacing without a corresponding increase in word spacing could slow reading. The study measured oral reading speed and accuracy, not text comprehension, and cannot justify a single typeface for all readers or all accessibility needs.

Lee et al. (2026) examined restructuring of LLM responses through ten interviews with people who reported attention-related challenges. Participants discussed condensation, hierarchy, and access to the original response. This qualitative evidence informs design requirements. It does not demonstrate a measured comprehension gain.

These studies address different populations and methods. The review does not combine them into a claim about “the neurodivergent reader.”

## 3. AI output: Understanding, confidence, and verification

### 3.1 Perceived clarity does not establish comprehension

Guo et al. (2026) evaluated plain-language summaries with 150 participants. Readers often gave AI-generated and human-written summaries similar clarity and coherence ratings, yet answered comprehension questions better after the human-written summaries. The authors also compared ten automated metrics with human judgments. Their results question the use of surface-quality scores as substitutes for reader outcomes. This review accessed the institutional record and abstract; it did not inspect the full experimental report.

Armstrong et al. (2026) compared three formats for AI-generated explanations of radiology reports in a within-participant study with 116 people. Sentence-paired and block translations both improved objective comprehension relative to a dictionary format: odds ratios 2.31 and 2.23, respectively. The abstract reports higher confidence for sentence pairing than for blocks; it does not establish superior objective comprehension for that contrast. This review accessed the published abstract.

The two studies test different interventions. One compares generated summaries with human summaries; the other compares presentation formats. Neither makes authorship alone a sufficient explanation of performance.

### 3.2 Presentation can change credibility

Anderl et al. (2024) ran two preregistered experiments with 556 and 666 participants. Conversational presentation increased credibility judgments relative to a static presentation of the information. The text-agent versus static comparison showed poorer discernment in the second experiment, not a significant difference in both. The manipulation concerned presentation mode; it does not establish that conversational wording alone caused the effect.

Steyvers et al. (2025) found that longer explanations increased confidence even when length did not improve answer accuracy. Explanations that communicated uncertainty in line with the model’s confidence narrowed calibration and discrimination gaps. The experiments used specified models and question tasks. They do not establish that a model’s verbal confidence provides a valid probability of truth.

### 3.3 Uncertainty wording is an intervention

Kim et al. (2024) conducted a preregistered experiment with 404 participants in a simulated medical-question task. First-person uncertainty reduced agreement and trust and improved accuracy. A more general uncertainty expression produced weaker effects. This evidence concerns tested phrases and conditions; it does not validate adding the same hedge to every answer.

For this report, uncertainty appears as a description of the evidence: sample, task, access limitation, comparison, or unresolved mechanism. That reporting convention avoids inventing a numerical confidence estimate. Its effectiveness in this document has not undergone a reader test.

### 3.4 Citations serve as evidence and as credibility cues

Ding et al. (2025) found that the presence of citations increased reported trust, including when citations were random. Their operational measure of citation checking recorded URL inspection by mouse hover, not proof that participants read the sources. The association between checking and trust does not establish that checking caused distrust.

Liu et al. (2023) audited four generative search systems. They reported that 51.5% of generated sentences had full citation support and that 74.5% of citations supported their associated sentence. These figures describe the systems at the time of that audit, not current products. The study separates citation presence from citation support.

This report links a source to the claim it supports and identifies the tested comparison. The reader should not need to infer that a cited paper supports every sentence in the surrounding discussion.

### 3.5 Response length remains an open optimization problem

Friedman et al. (2026) studied error detection with 24 professionals. Their workshop paper reports a pattern favoring medium-length explanations among erroneous outputs. The study did not hold length and content under a clean, balanced manipulation; the authors call for controlled length comparisons. The result does not establish an optimal word count.

Together with the confidence results above, this leaves two different questions: how much content supports correct understanding, and how presentation changes belief in correctness. The review found no validated universal target for answer length.

## 4. AI assistance: Performance and learning

### 4.1 Answer access can change what readers learn

Gajos and Mamykina (2022) conducted three experiments on nutrition decisions. Recommendations with explanations improved decisions without improving incidental learning. Requiring an initial decision before showing the AI did not solve that learning problem. Explanations without recommendations improved both outcomes in their experiments. The study concerns simulated AI and a defined task, not every generative assistant.

Bastani et al. (2025) studied nearly 1,000 high-school students. Standard GPT assistance improved practice grades by 48% relative to control, but unaided grades fell by 17%. A tutor configuration improved practice grades by 127%, without a detected unaided benefit. These are relative changes, not percentage points. The tutor combined several safeguards; the trial cannot isolate one instruction.

### 4.2 AI explanations can support learning

Liang et al. (2025) studied 458 participants on a sequence-prediction task. Participants completed 80 trials, and the researchers removed assistance after trial 40. Explanations improved learning and recovery after removal compared with the tested alternatives; benefits weakened on harder tasks. This is evidence from a controlled machine-learning task, not open-ended LLM document work.

Pan et al. (2025) conducted four experiments on AI-generated prequestions. Readers attempted questions before reading and then encountered the relevant information. Prequestioning improved test performance relative to reading alone or studying an outline, including for questions that differed from the practice questions. The experiments used short retention intervals and material within one topic. They do not establish long-term or far transfer.

The learning studies differ in the information readers receive and the activity they must perform. The findings do not support either “AI always harms learning” or “explanations always preserve learning.”

### 4.3 Forcing reflection can help, but the intervention matters

Buçinca et al. (2021) tested cognitive forcing functions with 199 participants. Some interventions reduced overreliance compared with explanation-based assistance, but the most effective designs received less favorable user ratings. The result identifies a tradeoff between measured reliance and reported experience.

De Jong et al. (2025) compared partial explanations, full explanations, and no explanations in graph and grammar tasks, with 264 and 210 participants. Partial explanations improved reliance relative to no explanations, but full explanations performed better than partial explanations. Removing explanatory content did not provide a general advantage.

The term “cognitive forcing” therefore names a family of interventions, not a verified instruction to withhold information.

### 4.4 Progressive disclosure: Promising interaction evidence, narrower learning evidence

Kim and Ji (2026) studied progressive disclosure and user initiative with 36 participants in exploratory search. Outcomes included perceived knowledge change, engagement, reported load, and search behavior. The study does not establish an objective learning gain from those subjective measures.

The result supports examining disclosure as an interaction design variable. A thesis that claims improved understanding must add a performance measure that tests understanding.

## 5. Agreements, tensions, and limits

| Question | What the retrieved evidence supports | What remains outside the demonstrated result |
|---|---|---|
| Does structure help? | Some headings and signals improve specified comprehension or learning outcomes | A universal hierarchy, section size, or heading count |
| Does simplification help? | Defined wording and syntax revisions can improve performance | Equivalence between a readability score and comprehension |
| Does emphasis help? | Lists can improve memory for selected items | Improved memory for the whole document |
| Do graphics help? | Meta-analyses show positive pooled effects for relevant graphics and integration | A benefit from every illustration; proof of one cognitive mechanism |
| Does fluent AI text help? | Readers can rate AI text highly | Correct comprehension or calibrated trust |
| Do explanations help? | Some explanation designs support learning or judgment | A benefit from explanation presence or length alone |
| Does effort help? | Certain questioning and forcing interventions improve specified outcomes | A rule that more effort or less information is better |
| Does AI improve work? | Assistance can improve task scores while available | Retention, transfer, or skill after assistance ends |

These are conditional statements tied to measured tasks. The literature includes apparent tensions because studies manipulate different features and measure different outcomes. A faster response, a higher confidence rating, and a better inference score answer different questions.

The evidence base also has transfer limits. Many text experiments use students and brief educational passages. Several AI studies use simulated assistants, constrained tasks, or specific model versions. The reviewed work does not establish one optimal format for long engineering proposals, mixed-expertise review groups, or repeated use over months.

## 6. Application to this report

The following choices implement the evidence without claiming that this report has undergone validation.

| Reporting choice | Evidence informing the choice | What the application preserves |
|---|---|---|
| Name the measured outcome in each finding | Outcome differences in Sections 2–4 | The distinction between comprehension, confidence, and learning |
| Use descriptive topic headings | Heading and signaling studies | Visible topic structure |
| Reserve tables for comparisons | Need to inspect intervention, outcome, and boundary together | Comparable fields without repeating whole narratives |
| Keep qualifications beside the result | Selective-recall findings for lists | Access to limits when a reader encounters the claim |
| State the actual comparator | Format and tutoring experiments | Protection against a stronger claim than the experiment tested |
| Separate findings from proposed tests | Differences between empirical evidence and transfer to a new task | The reader’s ability to assess extrapolation |
| Supply source identities and DOI links | Citation-support and citation-trust research | A route to verification |
| Preserve unresolved questions | Search and access limits | Visibility of what the review has not established |

These choices are reporting decisions. The cited studies did not test this exact combination. The report does not equate visual order with validated comprehension.

**Worked example of evidence-preserving revision**

A statement such as “AI tutoring improves learning by 127%” loses the distinction between supported practice and unaided performance.

A statement that preserves the trial’s contrast is:

> The tutor group scored higher during assisted practice. The study did not detect a higher score than control on the subsequent unaided exam (Bastani et al., 2025).

The revision retains the outcome, comparison, and limit. It does not require the reader to recover those distinctions from a separate limitations section.

## 7. Proposed evaluation protocol for a thesis

This section proposes a study design derived from the outcome distinctions in the reviewed research. It does not report a validated protocol.

**Research question.** For a defined reader group and task, which text and interaction features change comprehension, retention, and correct use of AI output?

**Material control.** Build variants from the same source material. Record which propositions, conditions, causal relations, examples, and qualifications each variant retains. Separate changes to content from changes to presentation. A shorter variant that removes a necessary condition tests both content deletion and length.

**Reader control.** Measure prior topic knowledge before exposure. Record the language and reading context relevant to the study. Define expertise through the task rather than a job title alone. If accessibility forms part of the research question, recruit and report the population concerned; do not substitute a broad identity category for a measured need.

**Intervention control.** Test separable features before testing a complete redesigned assistant. Possible contrasts include topic labels, causal links, list presentation, source pairing, answer disclosure, and prequestions. Use the same facts across presentation contrasts where the manipulation permits it. For a length contrast, specify which additional content creates the length difference.

| Question | Proposed measure | Safeguard against an invalid inference |
|---|---|---|
| Did the reader understand the relationships? | Questions that require inference or application | Avoid scoring phrase recognition as full comprehension |
| Did the reader retain the information? | Recall and application after a specified delay | Report the delay; do not call an immediate test durable learning |
| Did the reader use the information? | A task with a checkable answer or decision criterion | Separate aided and unaided phases |
| Did confidence track correctness? | Confidence on each answer paired with its correctness | Report confidence and accuracy together |
| Did structure change what readers missed? | Questions on central claims and qualifications | Test content outside the highlighted material |
| Did AI change reliance? | Responses to correct and incorrect assistance | Distinguish appropriate acceptance from acceptance alone |
| Did the design impose costs? | Completion time, abandonment, reported effort, preference | Do not substitute these for learning measures |
| Did citations support verification? | Source inspections and claim-support judgments | Distinguish opening or hovering from successful checking |

**Analysis.** Specify the primary outcome and contrasts before data collection. Report estimates and uncertainty for each contrast. Treat an inconclusive difference as inconclusive, rather than as proof of equality. Identify interactions with prior knowledge or task difficulty as planned or exploratory. Report losses as well as gains.

**Use of automated evaluation.** Automated checks can establish whether a variant retained a required proposition, preserved a condition, or cited an appropriate source. Those checks evaluate the artifact. A reader test evaluates the effect of that artifact. Passing the former does not establish the latter.

**A bounded first experiment.** Select one technical topic, one intended reader population, and one contrast: for example, a continuous explanation versus the same content with topic labels and explicit causal connections. Treat the combined intervention as a package unless a factorial design separates its features. Test inference accuracy, confidence in those answers, and retention after a stated delay. The design would answer a narrower question than “Which writing style fits human cognition?”

### 8.3 Evidence selection and trustworthiness

The review prioritizes original experiments, publisher records, author manuscripts, and institutional repositories. Meta-analyses inform claims about patterns across studies; original experiments identify interventions and comparators. Theory papers explain constructs but do not substitute for intervention evidence.

Access differed across papers. Full texts or author manuscripts informed the main appraisal of Kintsch, Cowan, Prinz-Weiß and König, Guo et al. (2020), Castro-Alonso et al., Anderl et al., Steyvers et al., Bastani et al., Pan et al., Friedman et al., and Galliussi et al. Publisher or institutional abstracts and indexed excerpts informed several other entries. In particular, the claims about Guo et al. (2026), Armstrong et al. (2026), de Jong et al., Liang et al., and Kim and Ji remain bounded by accessible abstracts or institutional records. No claim of a complete risk-of-bias assessment attaches to those records.

The review treats peer review as publication status, not proof of correctness. A meta-analysis provides broader coverage than one experiment, but its result depends on included tasks and measures. A qualitative study can establish reported experiences and design concerns without estimating a comprehension effect. The Friedman et al. paper provides preliminary workshop evidence.

The search included contrary and limiting results: no detected memory gain from headings, losses outside lists, limits to cognitive-load explanations, full explanations outperforming partial explanations, learning gains from AI-generated questions, and no detected unaided gain from a guarded tutor. These searches reduce one route to confirmation bias; they do not remove retrieval or selection bias.

This review did not register a protocol, search every bibliographic database, screen results with independent reviewers, or perform a new meta-analysis. Search-engine rankings and access constraints influenced the retrieved set. The search end date indicates when retrieval occurred, not complete coverage through that date.
