# A take-away after the lesson, for an AI agent that teaches one user

Research register · Search date: 10 November 2026

**Scope.** What evidence shows that a document the learner keeps after a lesson helps them retain and apply the topic later without AI. Such documents include a summary, reference sheet, crib sheet, study guide, job aid or review questions. The register also covers who should write it (the learner, the tutor, or both), what it should contain, and how an AI agent that teaches one user (the `teach-better` skill) should produce it. A syllabus or outline given before the lesson was in the first search and is out of scope. Section 2.6 keeps its findings for context.

**Status.** 13 questions closed: 9 answered, 2 contested, and 2 not found. Most evidence is old, small, or read only as abstracts. Many publisher pages were blocked. No study tested a take-away written by an AI tutor with a later test taken without AI.

The best-supported finding concerns how a take-away should be used, not the document itself. Answering questions about material beat restudying a review sheet six months later (Larsen et al., 2013a, 2013b). Restudying won only at a 5-minute delay, while it raised learners' confidence (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). A second finding concerns timing. Learners who expect to keep a record of information remember less of it (Sparrow et al., 2011). Students who expected to use crib cards scored lower on a surprise test without them (Funk & Dickson, 2011). Knowing a test was coming did not remove the harm, except for people who were made to offload everything (Grinschgl et al., 2021). Whether the learner or the tutor should write the summary is contested. In older studies, reviewing both the learner's notes and the instructor's notes did best (Kiewra, 1985).

## 1. What the documents are

- Terms overlap and are used loosely. Guided notes are "instructor-prepared handouts that provide all students with background information and standard cues", with spaces the learner fills in (Ohio State University, n.d.). Job aids "provide step-by-step instructions to be used in the workplace" (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). The research found no formal definition of a job aid, cheat sheet or study guide.
- A crib sheet is a page of notes that students are allowed to make before an exam and bring into it (Hamouda & Shaffer, 2015).

## 2. Evidence, with how strong it is

### 2.1. Rereading a summary against answering questions

**Evidence: consistent; read as abstracts.**

- Medical students who repeatedly took tests on a topic outperformed students who repeatedly studied a review sheet, six months later: "Repeated testing led to better long-term retention and application than repeatedly studying the material" (Larsen et al., 2013a).
- In a second randomized study, "learning through SP testing and written testing generally produced superior long-term retention compared to studying a review sheet" (Larsen et al., 2013b). SP means standardized patients.
- Restudying beat testing at 5 minutes. Testing won after 2 days and after 1 week, "even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material" (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
- Retrieval practice beat studying by building a concept map (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011).
- A review rated practice testing high in utility, and summarization and rereading low (Dunlosky et al., 2013).
- No study compared a study guide written as questions with one written as a summary. Putting review questions in a take-away is an inference from the studies above.

### 2.2. Expecting to keep a record

**Evidence: consistent in the lab; one classroom study.**

- "When people expect to have future access to information, they have lower rates of recall of the information itself and enhanced recall instead for where to access it" (Sparrow et al., 2011, abstract only).
- Items that people offloaded to an external aid were remembered worse once the aid was removed (Kelly & Risko, 2019, abstract only).
- Offloading harmed later memory even when people knew a test was coming. Participants "who were forced to offload maximally but were aware of the memory test could almost completely counteract the negative impact" (Grinschgl et al., 2021, abstract only).
- Offloading can also help: saving one file before studying a new one improved memory for the new file (Storm & Stone, 2015, abstract only). The effect held with printed lists (Flusberg & Ramos, 2018).
- In a classroom study, "Students' expectations of crib card use fostered dependency and reduced learning, while expectations of non-use did not" (Funk & Dickson, 2011).
- A review found the crib sheet literature "not decisive" on whether crib sheets help students perform or learn (Hamouda & Shaffer, 2015).

### 2.3. Notes and summaries given by the instructor

**Evidence: old and small.**

- "Students who review a detailed set of lecture notes from the instructor generally achieve more on exams than do learners who review their own notes, while learners who review both sets of notes perform best of all" (Kiewra, 1985).
- Under immediate testing, full instructor notes were "interfering" compared with the learner's own notes (Kiewra, 1985).
- Instructor notes helped on factual items, but "in no case were there differences with respect to higher order learning (application, analysis, synthesis, and problem solving)" (Kiewra, 1985). Kiewra suggests directives that make learners process the notes more deeply, as a hypothesis.
- Students recall "proportionately more of their own notes than those provided by the instructor" (Thomas, 1978, as reported by Kiewra, 1985).
- In one small experiment, students with their own notes or with skeletal notes outperformed students given full notes. Skeletal notes did not beat the students' own notes (Barnett, n.d.).
- A meta-analysis of note-taking interventions for college students, such as guided notes, reports a weighted average effect of 0.54 (Reed et al., 2016, abstract only).

### 2.4. Written by the learner or by the tutor

**Evidence: contested.**

- In a Grade 10 experiment, "Predefined summaries facilitated transfer performance compared to learner-generated summaries" (Leopold et al., 2013). The authors explain that learners who generate summaries "have to engage in behavioral processing but might not engage in deep cognitive processing."
- In introductory psychology, students' exam grades "are still higher when they create their own study guides" than with an instructor's concept list, though students preferred the instructor's list (Hackathorn et al., 2017; Cushen et al., 2019, abstract only).
- Writing summaries during an animated lesson beat no activity, one week later as well (Lawson & Mayer, 2024). This does not compare against a provided summary.
- Two meta-analyses compare each version only with its own no-aid control, not with each other. Learner-generated outlining gave g+ = 0.59 on memory and comprehension, and instructor-provided outlining gave g+ = 0.61 on memory and 0.34 on comprehension (Ponce et al., 2023, as quoted by Matuschak, n.d.-a). For graphic organizers, learner-generated gave g+ = 0.59 and 0.63, and instructor-provided gave 0.70 and 0.53 (Ponce et al., 2025, as quoted by Matuschak, n.d.-b). The full texts could not be read.
- Concept map use went with better retention, with effects from small to large depending on how maps were used (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006, abstract only).

### 2.5. Content, and leaving work for the learner

**Evidence: thin.**

- No primary study tested headings, worked examples or length in a take-away. Claims about them came only from study-skills guides, which the research did not use as evidence.
- Learners who received reduced explanations with prompts to infer the missing parts outperformed learners who received complete explanations with engaging prompts (Roelle et al., 2015). This suggests that leaving work for the learner in provided material helps. The study did not test notes or delayed transfer.

### 2.6. Out of scope: documents given before the lesson

**Evidence: thin.**

- Presenting learning objectives before each passage raised final-test scores. Pretest questions without feedback raised them more than plain objective statements (Sana et al., 2020).
- A learning-focused syllabus raised students' ratings of the course and instructor. The study did not measure learning (Wheeler et al., 2019, abstract only).
- `teach-better` already shows the GOAL and the plan up front (section 2.2), and already has a pre-questions tool.

### 2.7. Job aids and withdrawing them

**Evidence: indirect.**

- An editorial on a simulation study reports that "the higher level of performance was only maintained when the cognitive aid was present". The editorial adds that the result "could be interpreted quite differently", for example as the aid helping teams recall their training (Marshall, 2017).
- In a randomized trial, trainees performed better with a critical-event checklist present. The trial did not test performance once the checklist was withdrawn (Siddiqui et al., 2019, abstract only).
- In motor learning, frequent feedback during practice led to "extremely poor performance upon feedback withdrawal" (Park et al., 2000, abstract only). This is an analogy only.

### 2.8. Summaries written by an AI

**Evidence: essentially none.**

- No study tested a take-away written by an AI with a later test taken without AI.
- Medical students found no significant difference between AI-written and human-written summaries of abstracts, judged on readability and content quality (Matalon et al., 2024, n = 19).
- The nearest evidence concerns AI during practice. Unrestricted GPT-4 lowered later unaided grades by 17%, and a tutor with safeguards largely avoided the loss (Bastani et al., 2025, abstract only).

## 3. Where the evidence disagrees

- **Who writes the summary.** Leopold et al. (2013) favour a provided summary for transfer. Hackathorn et al. (2017) favour a self-made study guide for exam grades. Kiewra (1985) finds both together best.
- **Full notes.** Kiewra (1985) finds a detailed set of instructor notes helps on delayed factual tests. Barnett (n.d.) finds full notes did worse than the learners' own notes or skeletal notes.
- **Offloading.** Expecting a record harms memory for what it holds (Sparrow et al., 2011), but can free capacity for new material (Storm & Stone, 2015).

## 4. What this means for `teach-better`

The user later asked to optimise the skill for using the topic, not for recalling it. `sources/transfer-to-use-for-teaching.md` revises these points: practice and review use tasks from the user's own situations, and the take-away is a reference for use.

These are inferences from the evidence above, not tested rules.

1. **Give the take-away after the last quiz, and do not announce it earlier.** Expecting a record lowered memory for what it holds. Students who expected crib cards did worse without them. The skill's quizzes stay closed-book.
2. **Lead with review questions, with the answers kept apart.** Answering questions beat rereading a review sheet at delays of days to months. Restudying raised confidence without raising delayed recall.
3. **Ask the learner for their own summary first, as an option.** Writing it from memory is a retrieval attempt, and reviewing one's own notes together with the tutor's did best in Kiewra's studies. Who should write the summary is contested, so the agent writes one as well.
4. **Include a question that applies a rule to a new case.** Provided notes helped with facts, not with higher-order tasks.
5. **Keep the reference short, and leave work for the learner.** Full notes interfered on immediate tests in one study. Reduced explanations with prompts beat complete ones.
6. **Check it.** No study tested AI-written take-aways, and AI feedback contains errors (`sources/ai-agent-teaching-new-topics.md`, section 1.5). The learner will use the take-away without AI.
7. **Treat it as a design choice.** The format is not tested for adults learning alone or for AI tutors.

## 5. Method

- Research board: `.research/take-away-documents/` (not committed). It holds every question, claim and source, with each source marked by how it was read.
- Workers on a cheaper model researched the questions. Broadening searches included one in German. The coordinator checked 32 quotes against their sources, including every quote cited in sections 2.1 to 2.4 and 2.7. One quote could not be found on its page (Roelle et al., 2015, Experiment 2) and is not used.
- The user narrowed the scope to post-lesson documents during the research. Questions on pre-lesson outlines were already answered and are kept in section 2.6.
- The Ponce et al. (2023, 2025) effect sizes come from a notes page that quotes the abstracts, not from the papers.

## 6. Open questions

- **A study guide written as questions against one written as a summary.** None found.
- **A take-away written by an AI**, followed by a delayed test without AI. None found. Barcaui (2025), a trial reporting lower retention for ChatGPT users after 45 days, was blocked.
- **Job aids and later unaided performance.** Neal et al. (2012) and the original Everett et al. study were blocked or not found.
- **Head-to-head comparison of learner-written and provided summaries** with delayed tests. The Ponce et al. (2023, 2025) full texts were not read.
