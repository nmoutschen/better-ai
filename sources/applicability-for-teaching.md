# Teaching when to apply what was learned, for an AI agent that teaches one user

Research register · Search date: 11 November 2026

**Scope.** Why learners fail to use knowledge they can recall ("inert knowledge"), and which teaching moves help them recognise when to apply it. The register covers practice that asks whether a rule applies, comparison of cases, when to introduce technical terms, situated against generic first examples, factual pre-questions against application questions, and how well end-of-lesson test scores predict later use. It applies these to the `teach-better` skill. It extends `sources/transfer-to-use-for-teaching.md`.

**Status.** 15 questions closed: 8 answered, 2 contested, 4 not found, 1 out of scope. One low-priority question remains open. The coordinator checked 44 of 47 quotes against their sources. Most evidence comes from laboratory studies with text passages, artificial categories or school mathematics, often small. No study tested an AI tutor, and no study measured later use in the learner's own work.

Knowledge can be available and still go unused: learners can state it but do not apply it to problems where it is relevant (Whitehead, 1929; Renkl, 1994). Expert knowledge is "conditionalized": it specifies the contexts in which it applies (National Research Council, 2000). The best-supported move for building it is comparing two cases: learners who compared two analogs induced a schema that predicted transfer, while a stated principle with one case did little (Gick & Holyoak, 1983; Schalk et al., 2016). Questions train the kind of task they ask for: application quizzes helped definition questions, but definition quizzes did not help application questions (McDaniel et al., 2013), and pre-questions helped only the material they asked about (St. Hilaire et al., 2024). No study tested practice in deciding whether a rule applies to a learner's real tasks.

## 1. Evidence, with how strong it is

### 1.1. Inert and conditionalized knowledge

**Evidence: definitions and theory; German reviews read as abstracts.**

- Whitehead warned against "inert ideas", "ideas that are merely received into the mind without being utilised, or tested, or thrown into fresh combinations" (Whitehead, 1929).
- Experts' knowledge "is 'conditionalized' to specify the contexts in which it is applicable; it supports understanding and transfer (to other contexts) rather than only the ability to remember" (National Research Council, 2000).
- Renkl groups explanations of inert knowledge into three kinds: knowledge present but poorly controlled (meta-process), knowledge not stored in a form that can be applied (structure deficit), and knowledge bound to the situation where it was learned (situatedness). No single account suffices (Renkl, 1994, abstract only).
- Mandl, Gruber and Renkl attribute inert knowledge to abstract instruction, and report application problems among vocational and university students in business management (Mandl et al., 1993, abstract only).

### 1.2. Comparing cases

**Evidence: laboratory experiments, read in full.**

- With one story analog, summaries, a stated principle or a diagram did not help transfer. "If two prior analogs were given, subjects often derived a problem schema as an incidental product of describing the similarities of the analogs" (Gick & Holyoak, 1983). The quality of the schema predicted transfer.
- Teaching logic principles, "the combination of a generic formalism with a case was surprisingly unsuccessful while learners who compared two cases outperformed the control condition" (Schalk et al., 2016).
- A 16-week randomized classroom study taught second graders to represent the defining features of word-problem types, and found "superior word-problem learning" over conventional instruction (Fuchs et al., 2010). The authors argue that "the broader the schema or problem type, the greater the probability students will recognize connections between novel problems and those used for instruction and will understand when to apply the solution methods they have learned."
- Prompts to explain promoted transfer of a procedure in children, but did not improve a separate measure of conceptual knowledge (Rittle-Johnson, 2006).

### 1.3. Practice that asks whether a rule applies

**Evidence: thin. Small laboratory studies on artificial categories, and classroom interleaving studies.**

- In adult concept learning with five to ten participants per experiment, practice with examples alone did not produce concept learning. Adding nonexamples did, and nonexamples close to the examples (near misses) gave the most robust learning (Williams et al., 2025).
- A rule with examples and nonexamples taught concepts reliably; "after only practice, none of the participants demonstrated conceptual learning" (Williams & Roop, 2025, abstract).
- Interleaved practice, where the learner must pick the method from the problem, beat blocked practice in grade 7 mathematics, 72% against 38% (d = 1.05) (Rohrer et al., 2014, abstract only). The benefit depends on how similar the categories are (Carvalho & Goldstone, 2014, abstract only). See `sources/interleaved-practice-for-teaching.md`.
- No study tested practice in deciding whether a concept applies to real or realistic cases, outside laboratory categories, against later recognition of when to apply it.

### 1.4. When to introduce technical terms

**Evidence: contested; each side rests on one study or one summary.**

- In a first-year biology course, students who read a jargon-free text first, with terms given in class afterwards, scored the same on multiple-choice questions but gave "1.5 times and 2.5 times more correct arguments on two free-response questions about the concepts" (McDonnell et al., 2016, as reported by Weimer, 2017). The authors' own slides call the results preliminary and possibly topic-dependent (Barker et al., 2014). The primary paper was blocked.
- Against this, training in "the names and characteristics" of a system's key parts before a multimedia lesson improved problem-solving transfer "in five out of five tests", with a median d = 0.85 (Mayer, 2009, summary page only).
- The two lines differ in setting: Mayer's pre-training names parts of a system before a short animation; McDonnell et al. delayed field vocabulary in a reading. Both separate learning terms from learning the idea. No study tested the timing of terms for application in the learner's own tasks.

### 1.5. Situated against generic first examples

**Evidence: contested; mathematics laboratory studies with undergraduates.**

- Learners trained on an abstract instantiation "significantly and substantially outperformed the concrete condition on the transfer test" (d > 1.6) (Kaminski et al., 2008, as reported by Trninic et al., 2020).
- "If transfer to a new abstract domain is targeted, abstract instantiations are indeed more advantageous than concrete instantiations. However . . . Transfer to a new concrete domain is more enhanced by a concrete learning domain than by an abstract one" (De Bock et al., 2011, as quoted by Trninic et al., 2020).
- With better concrete examples, the abstract advantage disappeared: it arose "because the concrete condition employed suboptimal examples" (Trninic et al., 2020).
- Irrelevant context can hurt: students who saw a strategy "without a person were more likely to correctly transfer the novel strategy to new problems" than students who saw it tied to a person (Riggs et al., 2015).
- Problems matched to students' interests gave a small, significant gain on a later unit with standard problems (Walkington, 2012, conference paper). Training that resembles the work situation predicted transfer in a survey of 595 managers (van der Locht et al., 2013, abstract only).
- No study compared first problems from the learner's own work with generic ones, measured by later use.

### 1.6. Factual pre-questions and application questions

**Evidence: one preregistered meta-analysis and several experiments with text passages.**

- Prequestions had "a moderate specific effect (g = 0.54, k = 97) but a virtually nonexistent general effect (g = 0.04, k = 91)": they help the material asked about, not other material (St. Hilaire et al., 2024). The specific effect was marginally larger for factual (g = 0.58) than conceptual prequestions (g = 0.28).
- "Although factual pretesting improved learning for identical factual questions, conceptual pretesting did not enhance conceptual learning" (Hausman & Rhodes, 2018).
- "Post-testing also promoted knowledge transfer to previously untested questions, whereas pre-testing did not" (Latimier et al., 2019).
- In middle-school science, "Application questions increased exam performance for definitional-type questions and for different application questions. Definition questions did not confer benefits for application questions" (McDaniel et al., 2013, abstract only).
- Retrieving term-definition facts gave at best minimal transfer between term and definition (Pan & Rickard, 2017).
- One study of 74 eighth graders found no significant effect of question type or position on memory or application (Darwazeh & Reigeluth, 1982).
- No study gave pre-questions and then measured application to the learner's own tasks.

### 1.7. Test scores and later use

**Evidence: two meta-analyses of workplace training.**

- "The average decrease in effect sizes for the learning and behavioral comparisons was 0.77, a fairly large decrease" (Arthur et al., 2003). Learning criteria measure what trainees learned; behavioural criteria measure what they do at work.
- "Maximum and typical transfer were only weakly correlated" (Huang et al., 2015, abstract only). Knowledge and skill predicted what trainees can do; confidence, motivation and workplace support predicted what they do.
- Trained skills will not show at work "if incumbents do not have the opportunity to perform them" (Arthur et al., 2003).

## 2. Where the evidence is weak or disagrees

- **Settings.** Laboratory tasks, text passages, school mathematics and workplace training dominate. No study covered an AI tutor or an adult learning alone.
- **Terms.** Pre-training on names helped transfer in multimedia lessons (Mayer, 2009). Delaying field vocabulary helped free-response explanations in one biology course (McDonnell et al., 2016, as reported by Weimer, 2017). Both rest on thin reads.
- **Concrete and abstract.** Abstract training transferred better in one line of studies (Kaminski et al., 2008), but the advantage depended on the transfer target (De Bock et al., 2011) and on the quality of the concrete examples (Trninic et al., 2020).
- **Applicability practice.** Support for nonexamples comes from artificial categories with tiny samples. No study tested deciding whether a rule applies to a real task.
- **Bridging.** Salomon and Perkins (1989) describe deliberate abstraction and connection to new contexts as a route to transfer, but no experiment testing bridging prompts was found.

## 3. What this means for `teach-better`

These are inferences from the evidence above, not tested rules.

1. **Introduce terms where the user needs them, not as an opening glossary.** Give the idea in plain words, then the term, in the chunk that uses it. For a system with many named parts, a short card on the parts before the explanation is supported (Mayer, 2009). Teach a term as a goal only when the user must use it with other people.
2. **Take the first worked example from the user's situations,** when the user will apply the topic to concrete cases, and keep only the features the rule needs: irrelevant context hurts transfer. Once the user has practised one case, a second case to compare helps more than a stated rule with one case. `sources/analogical-comparison-for-teaching.md` found that novices learn more from one example at a time, so the comparison comes second.
3. **Practise deciding whether a rule applies.** Give short situations, including near misses where the rule does not apply, and ask the user to decide and say why. This is a design choice: no study tested it on real tasks.
4. **Use factual pre-questions only for facts the user must know without looking up.** Their benefit stays with the facts asked. Do not use them to prepare application.
5. **Ask application questions in quizzes.** They help definition questions too; definition questions do not help application.
6. **Report quiz results as what the user can do, not what they will do.** Learning scores overstate later use. Make feedback on a first real use the default ending.

## 5. Open questions

- **Practice in deciding whether a rule applies** to real tasks, against later use.
- **Timing of technical terms** for application, in adults.
- **First problems from the learner's own work** against generic ones, measured by later use.
- **Bridging prompts** that ask learners to connect a rule to their own contexts.
- **The empirical base** of the German inert-knowledge research (Gruber, Mandl, Renkl).
