# How to show versions of a design so a person can compare them

Research findings · Search date: 13 November 2026

Showing versions together, not one at a time, changes how people judge them, but only one study tests this on interface designs. Switching between two versions in the same place finds small differences at one spot faster than looking from one to the other. It loses that edge when layouts move or content differs, which is the usual case for design directions. The order of versions, their number, the scale they are shown at and the colour around them have no evidence specific to designs. For those, the comparison page rests on design choices.

**Scope.** How to present several versions of an interface, or a before and after, so that a person judges and chooses between them well: together or one at a time, switching in place, order and position, how many versions, scaling down, and the colour around them. The goal is to decide which features of the design-better comparison page (`compare.py`) rest on evidence, which stay as stated design choices, and which to drop.

**Status.** No high- or medium-priority question is open. Of 23 questions, 21 are answered, 1 found nothing, and 1 low-priority question was not researched. Ten Sonnet workers and one coordinator ran the research: 47 sources and 55 claims. Three Sonnet verifiers opened the source pages: 47 claims are verified and 8 are unverified, all 8 because the publisher blocked the page. The coordinator corrected 4 of the verified claims to match the passage the verifier copied. This report does not rely on the unverified claims; where a question's answer rests on them, the text says so. Many papers were read only as abstracts, and no study tested any of these questions on interface designs except Tohidi et al. (2006).

## 1. Together or one after the other

Users who saw an interface design among three alternatives rated it lower and gave more negative feedback than users who saw it alone (Tohidi et al., 2006). The authors read this as more honest ratings. No replication or critique of this study was found. Of 41 works citing it, the one extension found reports that the extra criticism appeared when the alternative kept the same task model but changed the abstract interface (Hammami et al., 2017).

In decision research, preferences can reverse between seeing options together and seeing them one at a time (Hsee, 1996). A 2024 replication with 310 participants reproduced the reversal only when both attributes were hard to evaluate (Weisgarber et al., 2024). Another study set out when the reversal appears and disappears (Schmeltzer et al., 2004). So the viewing mode changes the judgement, but this work does not say which mode is better.

Seeing two things side by side does not by itself make differences visible. Comparing two simple patterns within one fixation was as hard as comparing them over time, and pointing at the change made it easy (Scott-Brown et al., 2000).

**Challenge.** The challenge question found no contradicting evidence on interface designs. It found the limit above: joint viewing helps judge which version is better, and the decision literature does not show it predicts how one version is experienced alone (that point rests on a Wikipedia summary of Hsee and Zhang, 2004, and is not used as evidence).

## 2. Switching between versions in the same place

Observers found changes between two photographs faster when the photos alternated in the same place than when they were side by side (Josephs et al., 2016). The speed advantage held at small shifts between the images; at large shifts only an accuracy advantage remained. Radiologists reading mammograms were faster when toggling than when comparing side by side, even with imperfect alignment (Drew et al., 2016). The two studies come from the same research group. In brain scans, subtraction and automated change detection beat side by side, but the abstract does not separate alternation alone (Erickson et al., 2011).

For charts, a review of 15 user studies found that showing charts side by side almost never beat the other layouts for finding local differences (L'Yi et al., 2020). Item-to-item comparisons were less precise when charts were set apart than when they were overlaid or animated, while animation did worse for comparing correlations (Ondov et al., 2018).

**Challenge.** The challenge question found clear limits. In a 20-person study of immersive video, toggling failed when regions moved or content did not match, and participants preferred side by side for those tasks and for tasks with a high memory load (Wang et al., 2026). For judging image quality rather than spotting a change, an in-place method was unreliable for subtle differences, and its advantage over side by side was argued but not measured (Mohammadi et al., 2025). No study tested switching against side by side for screen designs, and design directions usually differ in layout, which is the case where switching loses.

## 3. Order and position

Position changes choices, but the direction depends on the setting. On California ballots, appearing first helped minor-party candidates in general elections and all candidates in primaries (Ho & Imai, 2008); in one election some candidates lost votes by appearing first (MIT Election Data + Science Lab, 2026). On restaurant menus, items at the start or end were chosen more than items in the middle (Dayan & Bar-Hillel, 2011). A review argues that one account explains these different directions (Bar-Hillel, 2015).

The usual remedy is to randomise or rotate the order, so that each option appears first for some people (Center for Civic Design, 2025), or to counterbalance order in user research (Sauro, 2015). These are practice, not tested fixes. A model of sequential choice predicts that clear quality differences and expertise change which position wins (Canic & Pachur, 2014); it is a simulation.

Studies of hotel lists, an 85-study review of ordering effects, and studies of a left-side bias in choices between pictures were found, but their pages were blocked, so they are not used here.

**Challenge.** Contradicting evidence comes from ballots only: no effect for major-party candidates in general elections, and a reversal for some candidates. No study of position among design versions was found.

## 4. How many versions

No study varies the number of design versions between 2 and 6. Across 50 consumer experiments, the effect of more options on choice averaged close to zero, with wide variation (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). A later meta-analysis found a significant effect once four moderators were included: how complex the set is, how hard the task is, how sure people are of their preference, and their goal (Chernev et al., 2015). A pre-registered experiment with 2 to 80 options found no effect on donations (Lindkvist & Luke, 2022). A preprint on drone designs found selection accuracy fell with 2, 8 and 16 options, but each number came with a different design problem (Xu et al., 2025).

## 5. Showing a design scaled down

No study compares judgements of the same design at different sizes. Larger displays raised attractiveness ratings of food pictures (Söderlund et al., 2019), and larger objects draw more eye fixations (Peschel & Orquin, 2013). Quick aesthetic judgements of websites rest on coarse visual structure, which survives scaling down (Thielsch & Hirschfeld, 2012). Low- and high-fidelity prototypes revealed substantially the same usability problems (Virzi et al., 1996).

## 6. The colour around the versions

The same grey looks lighter on a dark surround than on a light one (Soranzo et al., 2013). Brighter surrounds raised the smallest colour difference people could see on phone screens, most for light colours (Nikitenko et al., 2026). That study used outdoor light levels, not a page around a design. A trade-press summary says the ISO 3664 standard for judging colour asks for a neutral, matte surround (Printing Impressions, 2017); the standard itself was not read. The sRGB viewing environment uses a medium grey surround (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.), a secondary source.

## What this means for the comparison page

- Showing versions together has the support of one study on interface designs and of decision research. Section 1.5 of design-better can keep it, with the reason that people judge more critically when they see versions together.
- The one-at-a-time view helps only for small differences at the same place, such as a colour or spacing change in a before and after. For directions that differ in layout, side by side did better in the one study that tested moved content.
- Order, number of versions, scaling and default sizes have no design-specific evidence. They stay design choices, stated as such.
- A cream surround is not neutral, and surround changes perceived colour. The evidence is indirect, so this is a trade-off to report, not a reason to change the n14n colours.

## What the research does not cover

- No study tested any of these questions on interface designs, except Tohidi et al. (2006).
- ISO 3664 and ISO 12646 could not be read.
- The aerial-image study comparing flicker with side-by-side presentation (Springer, 2018) has no open copy.
- The individual studies in L'Yi et al. (2020) were read only through the review.
- Many sources were read as abstracts only, so effect sizes were not read.

## Open questions

- Low priority, not researched: what changes in ratings when the same picture is shown larger or smaller, in media-psychology experiments that randomise screen size.
