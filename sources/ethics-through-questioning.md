# Improving people's ethics by asking them questions

Research register · Search date: 7 November 2026

**Scope.** This report covers asking people questions as a way to improve their moral reasoning, ethical sensitivity, or conduct. It covers questions asked by a teacher, facilitator, coach, researcher, or the person themselves, and it excludes AI, which a previous report covered (`sources/ai-agents-moral-improvement.md`). The goal is to map which questioning approaches exist, what evidence says about when they work, and what risks sources raise, to inform how a question-based intervention might be designed.

**Status.** Questioning improves how people *reason* about ethics, but only by small amounts, and no study found here showed that it changes how they *act* over time. The effects depend on who is asked, which question is asked, and how the person answers. The same question can make one person more ethical and another person less ethical. Moral discussion seems to work best when the people in it build on each other's reasoning, rather than one side handing down answers. All high- and medium-priority questions are closed, many of them with gaps (section 7).

## 1. Four ways of using questions

The sources fall into four separate research fields, which rarely cite each other.

| Approach | Who asks, and what | Typical outcome measured |
|---|---|---|
| Socratic and dilemma discussion | A teacher or peers discuss moral dilemmas, often over several weeks | Moral reasoning scores, such as the Defining Issues Test (DIT) |
| Question-behavior effect | A researcher asks one question about future behavior ("Will you vote?") | Whether the person later does the behavior |
| Self-questioning and reflection prompts | The person answers prompts before deciding, or reflects on past experience | Choices in vignettes or lab tasks |
| Coaching and counseling | A coach, counselor, or ethics consultant asks questions one-to-one | Mostly not ethics: decisions, attitudes, or hospital outcomes |

The **Defining Issues Test (DIT)** is a standard questionnaire that scores how sophisticated a person's moral reasoning is. A higher score means reasoning more in terms of principles and less in terms of self-interest or rules.

## 2. Socratic and dilemma discussion improves reasoning modestly

### 2.1. The size of the effect

- **The classic synthesis** (Schlaefli et al., 1985) reviewed "55 studies of education interventions designed to stimulate development in moral judgment." It found that "the dilemma discussion and psychological development programs produce modest overall effect sizes," that "treatments of about 3 to 12 weeks are optimal," and that programs with adults aged 24 and older produced larger effects than programs with younger people. Only the abstract could be read, so the numeric effect sizes are missing.
- **A recent, broader synthesis** (Brown et al., 2023) covered 214 studies of character education and found "a small, significant average positive effect." Correcting for publication bias "indicated lower estimates of mean effects while remaining positive and significant." Kohlberg's Moral Dilemma Discussion was one of three programs with above-average effects. Character education includes much more than questioning, so this result is not specific to it.
- **Socratic questioning against lectures** (Torabizadeh et al., 2018). In a study of 103 nursing students, Socratic questioning improved moral reasoning more than a lecture did, and both beat a control group. *Unverified: only a database record was read, and the quote was not checked.*

### 2.2. A conflicting finding, mostly explained

A meta-analysis of research-integrity training (Katsarov et al., 2022) found that "primarily intellectual deliberation about ethical problems, often considered the 'gold standard' of ethics education, was significantly less effective." This looks like a contradiction of section 2.1, but the full text shows three differences:

1. **Definition.** Katsarov et al. call a course "deliberative" when it discusses concrete problems *without* addressing emotions. They compare it with "practical" courses that address both reasoning and emotions.
2. **Comparison group.** Deliberative courses did worse than practical courses. They were not shown to do worse than no course. The studies in section 2.1 compared discussion with a control group or a lecture.
3. **Population.** Higher-education students and non-research professionals learned less than high-school students and general citizens.

Across all course types, Katsarov et al. report a pooled effect of g = 0.41 on moral judgment (23 studies) and g = 0.69 on behavior (only 5 studies). The **effect size g** expresses the difference between groups in standard deviations, where 0.2 is usually called small and 0.5 medium. No source tested these three explanations directly.

### 2.3. What makes a discussion work

- **Transactive discussion.** A transactive statement responds to the other person's *reasoning*, not only to their conclusion. In a six-year program of studies, Berkowitz (1980) argued that "moral stage development results from discussion in which each member engages the reasoning of his/her discussion partners with his/her own reasoning."
- **A partner slightly ahead.** A widespread teaching convention says to expose students to reasoning one full stage above their own. Berkowitz (1980) found "significant development, but only for the subjects paired with a partner one-third of a stage above them." Partners at the same stage, or a full stage higher, did not produce gains. These were small dyad studies from the 1970s, so the finding is preliminary.
- **Peers rather than authorities.** Children produce their own transactive statements "proportionally more often with peers" than with adults (Kruger & Tomasello, 1986). With mothers, "moral judgments are experienced mostly as non-negotiable dictums, but with coequal peers they are experienced more as personal beliefs that can be actively negotiated" (Mammen et al., 2019). Both studies observed young children and did not measure later moral development.

No source read here tested open versus leading questions, or the skill of the facilitator.

### 2.4. Philosophy for Children

Philosophy for Children (P4C) teaches children to run their own philosophical discussions as a "community of inquiry." A systematic review of ten controlled studies found a mean effect size of 0.43, "a consistent moderate positive effect for P4C on a wide range of outcome measures" (Trickey & Topping, 2004). Those outcomes were mostly cognitive, such as reasoning and reading. A UK trial found that pupils "made approximately two additional months' progress in reading and maths" (Gorard et al., 2015; *unverified: the quote was not re-checked*), and a follow-up found "a small positive impact" on non-cognitive outcomes (Nuffield Foundation, 2017).

The evidence on moral outcomes is thin and conflicting. An Australian study of Year 6 pupils found "no change in pro-social behavior and emotional well-being" (Youssef et al., 2016). A study of 37 pupils in Iran reported improved moral judgment (Yaghoobi et al., 2026; *unverified*). Neither was randomized.

## 3. Reasoning gains are not shown to change behavior

No study found here tested whether a questioning or discussion intervention changed measured behavior at a later follow-up. The indirect evidence is weak:

- Across 50 studies, moral reasoning and ethical behavior correlate at r = 0.238 (Wu & Liu, 2014). A correlation of that size means reasoning explains about 6% of the variation in behavior.
- In one accounting course, "gains in moral reasoning ability are persistent, and are not diminished" three years later (Welton & Guffey, 2009). This study measured reasoning, not behavior.
- A meta-analysis of 25 business ethics programs found that they "have a minimal impact on increasing outcomes related to ethical perceptions, behavior, or awareness" (Waples et al., 2009).

## 4. A single question changes behavior a little

### 4.1. The question-behavior effect

Asking people whether they intend to do something makes them slightly more likely to do it. Across 116 tests, intention and self-prediction questions "have a small positive effect on behavior (d+ = 0.24)," with "larger effects for more desirable and less risky behaviors" (Wood et al., 2016).

Better-controlled studies find less. A second meta-analysis found an overall effect of g = 0.14, "with smaller effect sizes observed in more carefully controlled studies that exhibit less risk of bias" (Wilding et al., 2016). In studies at low risk of bias, the effect fell to g = 0.07.

For prosocial behavior, the estimates are contested:

| Source | Prosocial effect |
|---|---|
| Wood et al. (2016) | d = 0.19 |
| Rodrigues et al. (2016), reanalysing a different set of studies | d = 0.05 |
| Wilding et al. (2016), blood donation | g = 0.06 |
| Wilding et al. (2016), voting | g = 0.06, not significant |
| Gerber et al. (2003), field replication on voting | "No evidence was found that self-prophecy effects increase voter turnout." |

Rodrigues et al. (2016) conclude that "the evidence to date does not seem robust enough to provide implications for public health practice and policy." No study found here tested the effect on honesty.

### 4.2. Asking about the moral thing to do

Before an economic choice, Capraro et al. (2019) asked people "what do you personally think is the morally right thing to do in this situation?" In a crowdfunding study, "moral nudges increase donations by about 44 percent." This result comes from one campaign, and the outcome was giving, not honesty.

### 4.3. Asking about identity

The wording "don't be a cheater" reduced cheating more than "don't cheat" (Bryan et al., 2013). A registered replication reported: "In Experiment 1, we successfully replicated the results of the original experiment" (Guo et al., 2020, as listed by FORRT, n.d.). No failed replication was found, but neither paper could be read in full.

## 5. Questions can backfire

### 5.1. Questions that require an answer

Many companies give employees "contemplation questions," such as whether the company's reputation would suffer if a decision became public. In a working paper with 1,986 participants, "merely presenting CQs had no impact on ethical decisions" (Baader et al., 2025). Requiring written answers "led to marginally more ethical decisions among individuals with high moral identity but significantly fewer ethical decisions among those with low moral identity." **Moral identity** is how central being a moral person is to someone's sense of self. The authors warn of "promoting unethical behavior precisely in those already predisposed to such tendencies." The study used written scenarios, not real conduct.

### 5.2. Questions about vices

Asking people to predict a vice may increase it. People asked to predict whether they would skip class "engaged in the negative behavior (missing class) at a significantly greater rate" (University of Chicago Press Journals, 2007, reporting Fitzsimons et al., 2007). An earlier finding on drug use was challenged. Schneider et al. (2007) found that "the use of improper statistical tests and the presence of just a few extreme, outlying observations produced the illusion" of an effect, and that "removing them caused all tests of the treatment effect to become non-significant." The original authors replied that "the original warning cast by Williams et al. still stands" (Fitzsimons et al., 2007). An independent study of 1,988 students found that "surveying students to obtain information about past drug use did not increase risk for future student drug use" (Briney, 2017). Briney's survey asked about past behavior, not future behavior.

### 5.3. Reflection itself

Whether more time to think makes people more honest is contested. Shalvi et al. (2012) argued that "the dark side of people's automatic self-serving tendency may be overcome when time to decide is ample" (*unverified: the quote was not re-checked*). A preregistered replication "found that time pressure did not increase cheating" (Van der Cruyssen et al., 2020, as listed by FORRT). In a different task, "people are more honest under time pressure" (Capraro et al., 2018). None of these studies asked people questions, so they bear on questioning only indirectly.

## 6. Reflection prompts and coaching

### 6.1. Reflection prompts

- In a randomized trial with 74 nursing students, reflective practice raised moral sensitivity scores from 54 to 82 points (Zia et al., 2023). Scores dropped to 73 three months later but stayed well above the control group's 55. Problem-based learning did better than reflection on the same measure.
- Asking students to recall their past moral transgressions "effectively lowered moral self-concept, which in turn reduced dishonest behavior" in an incentivized cheating task (Keller & Szakál, 2025).
- For business managers, "application of material garnered from reflection on a personal experience was associated with decisions of higher ethicality" (Antes et al., 2012).

No randomized test of journaling or ethical checklists on real conduct was found.

### 6.2. Coaching and counseling

No source tested whether question-based coaching or counseling changes anyone's ethics. Clinical ethics consultation in intensive care units led more often to agreement on a clinical decision (Au et al., 2018, *unverified: the page was behind a cookie wall when checked*), but that measures the decision, not anyone's reasoning. Motivational interviewing and ethics coaching were not covered by any source read.

### 6.3. Objections to values clarification

Values clarification asks students to examine and state their own values, without the teacher judging them. Critics argue that, despite its claimed neutrality, it ends up "indoctrinating students in their position of ethical subjectivism and relativism" (Baer, 1982), and that it has been "characterized as amoral, subjectivist, inconsistent, and relativist" (Ryan, 1989). Both are opinion pieces, not studies. Critiques of manipulation in Socratic questioning appeared only in search results and were not read.

## 7. What the research could not find, and open questions

**Missing evidence:**
- A study showing that a questioning intervention changes ethical behavior at a later follow-up.
- Numeric effect sizes from the 1985 DIT meta-analysis, and results from the original Blatt and Kohlberg classroom studies (full texts blocked).
- Evidence on open versus leading questions, facilitator skill, or group versus one-to-one questioning.
- Any test of the question-behavior effect on honesty.
- Any outcome study of motivational interviewing, ethics coaching, or values clarification on ethics.
- A well-powered randomized trial of Philosophy for Children on moral or prosocial outcomes.

**Sources that could not be read** and could change parts of the answer:
- Leming (1981), *Curricular effectiveness in moral/values education: A review of research* (blocked).
- A study of moral awareness cues and cheating by Huynh, Stratmann and Rilke (blocked).
- Berkowitz and Gibbs (1983), and Walker and Hennig (1999), on transactive discussion and parents' questioning (blocked).
- The full reply by Fitzsimons et al. (2007), which reports their own replications (blocked).

**Open low-priority questions:**
- Which terms each field uses, and what each refers to.
- Perspectives in non-English and non-Western literature, such as Confucian dialogue traditions.
